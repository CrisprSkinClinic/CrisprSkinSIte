Starting a biologic is a big decision. Knowing the path helps you plan.

## Before the First Dose

- **Assessment** of how severe the psoriasis is and how it affects your life.
- **Screening** for tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C and, where relevant, HIV, plus routine blood tests.
- **Vaccines:** ideally completed beforehand. See [vaccines before immunosuppressants](/blog/vaccines-before-immunosuppressants-biologics-checklist).
- **Choice of medicine,** based on your health, joint symptoms, pregnancy plans and convenience.
- **Teaching** you or a family member to inject.

## Weeks 0 to 4

The first injections are given, sometimes with extra loading doses. Mild redness at the injection site, tiredness or headache are common. Some people notice itching and scaling ease within the first weeks.

## Months 1 to 3

Plaques flatten and fade. A good response by week twelve to sixteen usually means a large drop in extent and severity. A review is arranged at about three months.

## Months 3 to 6

Skin often reaches its best, and many people are almost clear. If response is partial, the dose, spacing or the medicine can be changed.

## Months 6 to 12

Maintenance: injections continue at regular intervals, with check-ups every few months and blood tests where needed. Joint and nail symptoms may improve too.

## What If It Does Not Work?

About one in four people do not respond well to a first biologic, or lose response over time. Switching to a different class or another drug in the same class is common and often successful.

## Staying Safe

Report fever, persistent cough, wounds that do not heal, or any infection promptly, and tell any doctor or dentist that you are on a biologic. Avoid live vaccines. See [biologics: safety, TB screening and monitoring](/blog/biologics-safety-infections-tb-screening-and-monitoring).

## Life on Treatment

Many people stay on a biologic for years. Some reduce or stop when skin is clear for a long time, with a plan agreed with the doctor.
