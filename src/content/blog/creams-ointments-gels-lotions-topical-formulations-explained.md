The same medicine often comes as a cream, an ointment, a gel, a lotion or a foam. They are not interchangeable. The base, called the vehicle, decides how well the medicine gets in, how it feels, and which part of the body it suits.

## Ointment

An ointment is mostly oil (such as petroleum jelly) with very little water. It is greasy and sticky, seals in moisture, and lets more medicine into the skin. It is best for **dry, thick, scaly or cracked skin**, such as eczema on the hands and feet, psoriasis plaques and heels. It stings less on broken skin and needs fewer preservatives. Avoid it on hairy areas, folds that sweat a lot, and acne-prone skin, where it can block pores.

## Cream

A cream is a mix of oil and water. It spreads easily, absorbs well and feels less greasy. It is the most commonly prescribed form and suits **most areas of the body**, including the face, trunk and limbs. Creams may contain preservatives that occasionally irritate sensitive skin.

## Lotion

A lotion is thinner, with more water, so it spreads over large and **hairy** areas and is easy to apply on the scalp and the back. It is less moisturising than a cream, and may sting inflamed skin.

## Gel

A gel is clear and water- or alcohol-based. It dries quickly, feels light, and suits **oily, acne-prone skin**, the scalp and hairy areas. It can sting or dry the skin, so it is less suited to dry or cracked skin.

## Solution, Foam and Spray

Thin liquids and foams reach the **scalp** and hairy skin without leaving residue. Minoxidil, many steroid scalp products and some acne medicines come this way. Alcohol-based versions can sting.

## Paste, Powder and Shampoo

Pastes stay in place and are used for specific patches. Powders keep **folds and feet** dry in fungal infections. Medicated shampoos work as a short-contact wash, usually left on for a few minutes before rinsing.

## Why the Form Matters

A steroid in an ointment is stronger than the same steroid in a cream, because more passes through the skin. Using a lotion on dry, cracked skin may sting and do little. Using a heavy ointment on an oily face may cause breakouts.

## Picking the Right One

Match the form to the skin: dry and thick, ointment; normal skin and most areas, cream; hairy or scalp, lotion, gel or foam; oily and acne-prone, gel. Follow the form your doctor prescribed, and ask before switching.

## How Absorption Differs

The vehicle affects how much medicine enters the skin. Ointments hydrate the outer skin and let more medicine pass. Creams are in between. Lotions and gels deliver less, but spread over large areas. Skin thickness matters too: the face, eyelids, groin and armpits absorb far more than the palms and soles. This is why a steroid may be safe on the leg, but not on the face, and why doctors choose strengths and forms carefully.

## Matching Form to Common Conditions

- **Eczema on the hands and feet:** ointment or thick cream.
- **Weepy eczema or rashes in skin folds:** lotion or a cream, as ointments trap heat.
- **Scalp psoriasis or dandruff:** shampoo, lotion, solution or foam.
- **Acne:** gels or lightweight creams.
- **Fungal infection in folds:** cream or powder.
- **Cracked heels and thick plaques:** ointment, often with urea or salicylic acid.
- **Sunscreen:** gel or fluid for oily skin, cream for dry skin.

## Reading Labels

The label may list the active ingredient, its strength as a percentage, and the base. Ingredients that are listed first are present in largest amounts. A product labelled "fragrance-free" is not the same as "unscented", since masking agents may be present. If you have a known allergy, take the product to your doctor.

## Storage and Expiry

Keep tubes closed, in a cool place, away from sunlight and bathroom steam. Do not dip fingers into jars, since contamination spoils the product. Medicated creams have shorter life after opening, so check dates. Discard old and unlabelled tubes.

## Mixing Products

Do not mix prescribed medicines with moisturisers or other creams unless your doctor tells you to, since dilution may change strength and stability. If you use more than one product, apply the prescribed medicine first, wait a few minutes, then the moisturiser, unless told otherwise.

## Allergy and Irritation

Ingredients such as preservatives, fragrances and certain antibiotics sometimes cause allergy. If skin becomes more red, itchy or blistered after a product, stop it and see your doctor. See [contact dermatitis](/conditions/contact-dermatitis).
