Many people with eczema find that skin gets worse when the weather changes. Dry air weakens the skin barrier, which lets irritants in and moisture out.

## Why Winter Is Harder

Low humidity, hot showers, heaters, air-conditioning and woollen clothes all irritate eczema-prone skin. Infections such as colds can trigger flares too.

## Calming a Flare

- Moisturise generously, at least twice a day, with a thick, fragrance-free cream or ointment.
- Bathe briefly in lukewarm water.
- Use the medicated cream your doctor has prescribed on affected patches, exactly as advised.
- Avoid scratching, and keep nails short.
- Choose soft cotton next to the skin.

## Know Your Triggers

## When to See a Doctor

Book a visit if the skin is weeping, crusted or painful, if itching disturbs sleep, or if creams have not helped in two weeks. Infected eczema needs treatment.

## Recognising a Winter Flare

Winter eczema usually appears as dry, rough, red or darker patches that itch, often in the creases of elbows and knees, on the hands, neck, eyelids and around the mouth. Skin may crack, and scratching can cause weeping or crusting. Many people also notice that the skin looks "scaly" and tight on the shins.

## What Makes It Worse

- Dry air from air-conditioning and heaters.
- Hot showers and prolonged soaking.
- Woollens and synthetic fabrics against the skin.
- Scented soaps, detergents and fabric softeners.
- Viral infections such as colds, which can trigger flares.
- Lack of sleep, stress and sweat followed by cold air.

## A Plan for the Cold Season

**Daily:** moisturise twice, more on hands after washing. Use soap-free wash.

**At the first sign of a flare:** start the medicine your doctor has prescribed, often a short course of a steroid or non-steroid cream, early. Starting early works better than waiting for severe flares.

**Wet wrap therapy:** for severe patches, a doctor may recommend applying medicine and moisturiser under damp bandages or clothing for a short period. This should be taught by a professional.

**Keep nails short and smooth.** Cotton gloves at night help children and adults who scratch in their sleep.

## What About Antihistamines?

Antihistamines do not treat eczema itch directly, though a sedating one at night may help sleep for a short period. Do not rely on them in place of treating the skin.

## Infection Warning Signs

Yellow crusts, oozing, pain, fever, or clustered punched-out blisters suggest infection and need prompt care.

## When Eczema Is Not Settling

Ask about treatment options beyond creams, including phototherapy and newer medicines, particularly if eczema affects work, sleep or school. ## Prevention Through the Year

Winter flares are often easier to prevent than to treat. Build the moisturiser habit in autumn, use a mild cleanser all year, and keep a record of triggers. See [identifying eczema triggers](/blog/eczema-trigger-identification).
