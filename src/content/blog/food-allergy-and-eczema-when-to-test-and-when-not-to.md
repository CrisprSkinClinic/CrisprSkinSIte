Parents of children with eczema are often advised to remove eggs, milk, wheat or nuts. In most cases, that is unnecessary.

## Who May Have a Food Allergy

A minority of young children with moderate or severe eczema have a true food allergy, most often to egg, cow's milk or peanut. The clues are a clear reaction soon after eating a food, such as hives, swelling, vomiting or a sudden flare within hours. Eczema that worsens slowly, over days, is rarely due to food.

## Adults

Food is rarely the trigger for adult eczema. Irritants, dry skin, stress and infection are more common.

## When to Test

Testing is worthwhile when there is a clear, repeatable reaction, or in infants with severe eczema and early-onset concerns. Tests only suggest sensitisation, and a positive result without symptoms does not prove allergy. An allergy specialist may arrange a supervised food challenge.

## The Harm of Unnecessary Elimination

Removing foods without proof can cause poor nutrition and growth problems, and may increase the risk of later allergy. Current advice is not to avoid common allergens without a reason, and to introduce them in infancy, with advice for high-risk babies.

Regular moisturiser, treating flares promptly and avoiding irritants. See [managing childhood eczema](/blog/managing-childhood-eczema) and [childhood atopic dermatitis](/conditions/childhood-atopic-dermatitis).

## The Link Between Eczema and Allergy

Many children with eczema also have a tendency to other allergic conditions, such as asthma, hay fever and food allergy, a pattern called the atopic march. Having eczema, particularly early and severe, increases the chance of food allergy. This does not mean food causes the eczema. The reverse may be partly true: a damaged skin barrier may allow food proteins to enter the skin and cause sensitisation. This is why treating eczema early and keeping skin moisturised matters.

## Early Introduction of Allergenic Foods

Evidence from trials shows that, in infants at high risk, introducing peanut early, around four to six months, under medical advice, lowers the risk of peanut allergy. Similar guidance applies to egg. Infants with severe eczema or an existing egg allergy should be assessed before introduction. For most babies, allergenic foods can be introduced at the same time as other solids. Do not delay them without a medical reason.

## How Food Allergy Presents

- **Immediate reactions:** within minutes to two hours. Hives, swelling of the lips and face, vomiting, wheeze, or collapse. Needs emergency care.
- **Delayed reactions:** eczema flares hours to days later, diarrhoea, or poor weight gain. Harder to link and less certain.

## Tests, and Their Limits

Skin-prick tests and blood tests measure sensitisation, not allergy. Many children have positive results but eat the food without problems. A positive test with a convincing history supports allergy. A supervised food challenge in a specialist centre is the most reliable way to confirm or exclude it.

## The Dangers of Home Elimination

Restricting milk, egg, wheat and other staples can lead to poor growth, nutrient deficiency and, ironically, a higher risk of developing true allergy later. Mothers who are breastfeeding should not routinely avoid foods unless advised by a doctor. Alternative milks may lack adequate nutrition. If you suspect a food, bring your concern to your paediatrician or dermatologist.

## What Improves Eczema Most

Regular moisturising, appropriate anti-inflammatory creams used early, avoiding irritants, treating infections and good sleep. For difficult cases, see [moderate to severe atopic dermatitis](/conditions/moderate-to-severe-atopic-dermatitis) and [newer treatments for stubborn eczema](/blog/newer-treatments-for-stubborn-eczema).

## Adults

In adults, food is rarely an eczema trigger, but can cause contact reactions around the mouth, or in people with certain hand eczema, for example from handling spices or raw vegetables. Patch testing can help in such cases.
