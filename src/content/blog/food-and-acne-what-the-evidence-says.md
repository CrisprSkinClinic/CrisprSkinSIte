Almost every patient with acne has been told to cut out something. Here is what research supports.

## Better Evidence

- **High-glycaemic foods:** sugary drinks, sweets, white bread and refined snacks are associated with more acne, and small trials of lower-glycaemic diets show modest improvement.
- **Dairy:** an association exists, mainly with skimmed milk. It is not proven that dairy causes acne in everyone.
- **Whey protein supplements:** often linked to acne flares in people who train.

## Weaker or Unclear

- **Chocolate:** results are mixed, and some studies suggest a link in sensitive people.
- **Oily and fried food:** eating oil does not make skin oily in a direct way, though poor overall diet may matter.
- **Omega-3, probiotics and zinc:** some small studies suggest benefit, but not enough to replace treatment.

## What This Means in Practice

A balanced diet with fewer sugary drinks and refined carbohydrates is sensible. If you notice that a particular food makes you break out, try cutting it for a few weeks and see. Do not cut whole food groups.

## Diet Does Not Replace Treatment

Acne is driven by hormones, oil, blocked pores and inflammation. Diet changes help at the margins, and treatment does the main job. See [acne treatment](/services/acne), [why acne treatment takes time](/blog/why-acne-treatment-takes-time), and [hormonal acne](/conditions/hormonal-acne).

## Protein Powders

If you use whey and have stubborn acne, stop it for two months and see. Ask your dermatologist for advice. See [diet and skin overview](/blog/diet-and-skin-what-evidence-actually-shows).

## Looking at the Studies

**Glycaemic load:** several randomised trials have compared low-glycaemic-load diets with higher-glycaemic ones in people with acne. Most found that lowering glycaemic load reduced lesion counts, although trials were small and short. The proposed mechanism involves insulin and IGF-1, which stimulate oil glands and keratinocytes.

**Dairy:** large cohort studies, especially in adolescents and young women, link regular consumption of milk, especially skimmed milk, with more acne. Findings with yoghurt and cheese are less consistent. Curd and buttermilk, common in Indian diets, have not been well studied.

**Whey protein:** case reports and series describe acne flares in gym-goers, particularly young men, after starting whey supplements. These may respond to stopping the supplement.

**Chocolate and fat:** early studies were flawed. More recent trials suggest high sugar content, not cocoa itself, may be the issue.

**Fish oil and omega-3:** small studies show some benefit, but not enough to recommend as treatment.

**Zinc:** some trials show benefit as an addition, but results are mixed and high doses cause nausea.

## A Practical Plan

1. Reduce sugary drinks, sweets and large amounts of white rice and bread at one sitting, replacing them with whole grains, pulses and vegetables.
2. If you take whey protein, try stopping it for eight weeks.
3. If you drink a lot of milk, consider reducing it and watch for change, but keep adequate calcium and protein.
4. Do not cut out entire food groups or start restrictive diets without guidance.

## Diet in Context

Acne is driven by hormones, genetics, oil production, bacteria and inflammation. Diet is one smaller factor. Dermatologists treat acne with medicines because they work. See [acne treatment](/services/acne), [hormonal acne](/conditions/hormonal-acne) and [why acne treatment takes time](/blog/why-acne-treatment-takes-time).

## Eating Disorders and Acne

Cutting back extremely on food in a quest for clear skin is harmful. If you notice that you are preoccupied with food rules, seek help.

## What About Supplements?

Many acne supplements are marketed with limited evidence. Talk to your doctor before starting, particularly about zinc, vitamin A, and herbal products, which can interact with acne medicines such as isotretinoin.

## Keeping a Diary

If you suspect a link, record what you eat and your skin each day for four weeks. Patterns emerge more clearly than memory suggests, and discourage unnecessary restrictions.
