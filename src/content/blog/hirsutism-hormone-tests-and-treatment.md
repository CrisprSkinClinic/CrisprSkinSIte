When excess hair is more than expected, the aim is to find why. This guide outlines the usual path.

## Tests

Your doctor may check total and free testosterone, DHEAS, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, prolactin and thyroid tests, often taken early in the menstrual cycle, and blood sugar and cholesterol. A pelvic ultrasound may be advised. Many women need only some of these.

## Common Causes

PCOS is by far the most frequent. Less often, non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia, thyroid or prolactin problems, medicines, or an unexplained increase in sensitivity of hair follicles to normal androgen levels. Tumours are rare.

## Medical Treatment

Treatment is chosen by your doctor and may include a combined oral contraceptive, an anti-androgen such as spironolactone or cyproterone, and metformin where insulin resistance is present. Eflornithine cream can slow facial hair. These are prescription medicines with real benefits and real cautions.

## Important Cautions

Anti-androgens can harm an unborn baby, so effective contraception is part of treatment. Medicines are not suitable for everyone, and should not be started without assessment.

## How Long

Hair takes months to respond. Expect six to twelve months for a clear difference, and continued treatment to maintain it. Combine it with [laser](/blog/hirsutism-laser-and-medical-treatment-together) to remove existing hair.

## When Are Tests Needed?

Many doctors check hormones in women with moderate to severe hirsutism, or with mild hirsutism together with irregular periods, acne, scalp thinning, rapid onset, or signs of virilisation. A woman with mild, long-standing hair, regular periods and no other signs may need few or no tests.

## How the Tests Are Timed

Hormone levels vary through the menstrual cycle. Many are best drawn in the early follicular phase, around days two to five. Some medicines, such as oral contraceptives, affect results, and your doctor may advise a pause. Tell your doctor which medicines you take.

## What Each Test Tells Us

- **Total and free testosterone:** raised in PCOS and ovarian causes.
- **DHEAS:** produced by the adrenal glands. Very high levels point to an adrenal source.
- **17-hydroxyprogesterone:** screens for non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia.
- **Prolactin:** high levels can cause irregular periods and hirsutism.
- **TSH:** thyroid disease affects periods and metabolism.
- **LH, FSH and AMH:** sometimes used in PCOS assessment.
- **Glucose, HbA1c and lipids:** since PCOS is linked with insulin resistance.

## The Medicines in More Detail

**Combined oral contraceptive pills:** reduce ovarian androgen production and raise a binding protein, lowering free testosterone. Often first-line in women not planning pregnancy.

**Spironolactone:** blocks androgen receptors. Typically used with contraception, and with monitoring of potassium and blood pressure when needed.

**Cyproterone acetate:** a strong anti-androgen, often combined with an oestrogen. Use is restricted by safety concerns, and doses and duration are carefully planned.

**Metformin:** helps women with insulin resistance and PCOS, with modest effect on hair.

**Finasteride:** occasionally used in selected women under specialist care, and never in women who could become pregnant.

**Eflornithine cream:** applied to unwanted facial hair, with continuous use.

## Safety Points

Anti-androgens can feminise a male foetus, so reliable contraception is essential. Some medicines raise clot risk in women with certain risk factors, so a medical history is needed. Blood pressure, weight and lipids are monitored.

## Why Patience Matters

A hair follicle takes months to cycle. Medicines reduce new hair growth, and visible change takes six to twelve months. Stopping treatment usually allows hair to return, so long-term plans are common.
