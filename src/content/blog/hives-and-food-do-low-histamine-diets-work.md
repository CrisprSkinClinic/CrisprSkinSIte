Hives often make people suspect food. In reality, food is the cause in only a small share of cases.

## Acute Hives After Food

If hives, lip swelling or breathlessness appear within an hour or two of eating a specific food, and happen again, food allergy is likely. Avoid that food and get assessed, because reactions can be serious.

## Chronic Hives

Hives lasting more than six weeks are usually not due to food. Most have no identifiable trigger. Our guides to [when to worry](/blog/hives-when-to-worry) and [hives not responding to antihistamines](/blog/hives-not-responding-to-antihistamines) cover what does help.

## Low-Histamine and Pseudo-Allergen Diets

These restrict foods that contain or release histamine, such as fermented foods, tomatoes, spinach, aged cheese, and some additives. A few small studies show benefit in some people, but evidence is limited and mixed, and the diets are hard to follow.

## If You Want to Try

Do it under guidance, for a limited trial of two to three weeks, with a diary. If there is no clear benefit, stop and return to normal eating.

## Other Triggers

Infections, certain medicines such as aspirin and anti-inflammatory painkillers, heat and stress are more common triggers.

## When to See a Doctor

See a doctor for hives lasting more than a few days, or any lip, tongue or throat swelling. Read about [urticaria](/services/urticaria) and the [overview of diet and skin](/blog/diet-and-skin-what-evidence-actually-shows).

## Types of Food Reactions in Hives

**IgE-mediated food allergy:** hives, swelling or other symptoms within minutes to two hours of eating the same food, repeatedly. Common triggers include nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk, wheat and certain fruits. Testing and avoidance guided by an allergist are appropriate.

**Cross-reactive and exercise-related reactions:** for example, hives after eating wheat or shellfish and exercising, or oral itching with raw fruits in people with pollen allergy.

**Pseudo-allergic reactions:** triggered by food additives, aspirin-like compounds and histamine-rich foods in some people with chronic hives, without classic allergy.

**Coincidence:** most cases. Because we eat several times a day, it is easy to blame the last meal.

## Understanding Histamine in Food

Histamine is found in fermented and aged foods, such as cheese, wine, vinegar, fermented soy, cured meats and some fish that has been stored poorly, and some vegetables, such as tomato, spinach and aubergine. Some foods also release histamine from the body's own cells. Most people break down dietary histamine easily with an enzyme in the gut. Whether low enzyme activity causes chronic hives is not established.

## What the Studies Show

Small studies of low-histamine and pseudo-allergen-free diets find some improvement in a subset of people with chronic hives, but results vary and are difficult to repeat. Few well-controlled trials exist. Diets are demanding, and may lead to nutritional gaps if kept up for a long time.

## If You Try a Diet

- Do it for a limited period, two to three weeks, with a symptom diary.
- Reintroduce foods one at a time to see which matter.
- Do not stop prescribed treatment.
- Involve a dietitian when possible.

## Medical Treatment Works

Most chronic hives respond to non-sedating antihistamines, taken regularly, at higher doses if needed under guidance. For stubborn cases, injectable treatments exist. See [hives not responding to antihistamines](/blog/hives-not-responding-to-antihistamines) and [antihistamine-resistant urticaria](/conditions/antihistamine-resistant-urticaria).

## Other Common Triggers

Infections, stress, heat, pressure from clothes, painkillers such as aspirin, ibuprofen and diclofenac, and in some people alcohol. Check medicine labels.

## When Food Allergy Is Dangerous

Hives with throat tightness, wheeze, repeated vomiting, dizziness or fainting is an emergency. Carry the adrenaline auto-injector if prescribed, and seek immediate help.
