HPV is a large family of viruses. A few types cause genital warts, a group of high-risk types causes cervical cancer and some cancers of the vulva, anus and throat, and other types cause the common warts on hands and feet.

## What the Vaccine Does

It prevents infection with the HPV types it covers, which are the ones behind most genital warts and most HPV-related cancers. It works best before any exposure.

## What It Does Not Do

It does not clear an infection you already have, and it does not treat warts. It is also not aimed at the common warts of hands and feet, which are mostly caused by other types. Those are covered on our page for [viral warts](/conditions/viral-warts).

## Who Should Consider It

Girls and boys from 9 years, with the best response in the early teens. Two doses are enough when started at 9 to 14 years. From 15 years, or with weakened immunity, three doses are used. Adults, both men and women, can benefit too, after a conversation about their situation.

## Before Immunosuppressants

If you are about to start medicines that lower immunity, completing the course first is sensible. See our [pre-treatment vaccine checklist](/blog/vaccines-before-immunosuppressants-biologics-checklist).

## Screening Still Matters

The vaccine does not replace cervical screening where it is recommended.

## Questions We Hear Often

**Does the vaccine cause infertility or encourage risky behaviour?** No. Large studies across many countries have found no effect on fertility, and no increase in sexual activity after vaccination. It is a cancer-prevention vaccine, studied for safety in millions of doses.

**Is it only for girls?** No. Boys and men benefit too, since HPV causes genital warts and cancers of the anus, penis and throat in men, and vaccinating them also helps reduce spread.

**I am over 26. Is it too late?** Not necessarily. The benefit is greatest before exposure, but adults who have not been infected with every vaccine type can still gain protection. The decision is personal, and we discuss your situation.

**What if I already have genital warts or an abnormal smear?** The vaccine does not treat them. Existing infection needs examination and treatment. Vaccination afterwards may still protect against other covered types, so ask your doctor.

**Do I still need cervical screening after vaccination?** Yes. The vaccine does not cover every cancer-causing type, so screening continues as recommended.

## Doses and Schedule

When vaccination starts between 9 and 14 years, two doses given about six months apart are enough. From 15 years, and in people with weakened immunity, three doses are given over six months. A missed dose can be completed without starting again. Your doctor will plan the dates with you.

## Side Effects

The commonest effects are a sore arm, mild redness or swelling, a headache or a day of tiredness. Fainting after any injection is more common in teenagers, so we ask them to sit for fifteen minutes afterwards. Serious allergic reactions are rare.

## HPV, Skin and Warts

Several things get called "HPV". Common warts on the hands and feet and flat warts on the face are caused by different types from those targeted by the vaccine. Genital warts are caused mostly by two types that the vaccine does cover. If you are not sure which kind you have, an examination clarifies it. See our page on [viral warts](/conditions/viral-warts).

## Before Immunosuppressants

People who are about to start biologics, methotrexate or other medicines that lower immunity have a higher risk of persistent HPV infection and related problems. Completing the HPV vaccine beforehand is sensible, and the three-dose schedule is used. We plan the timing alongside your treatment.
