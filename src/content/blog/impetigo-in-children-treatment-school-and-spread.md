Impetigo looks worse than it is, and it clears quickly with treatment. The challenge is stopping it from passing between children.

## What It Looks Like

Small red sores or blisters that burst, leaving honey-coloured or yellowish crusts, often around the nose and mouth, on the face and on the arms and legs. It may itch. In babies, large blisters can appear (bullous impetigo).

## Why It Happens

Bacteria (Staphylococcus or Streptococcus) invade skin that is broken: by scratches, insect bites, eczema, scabies or a runny nose. It thrives in warm, humid weather. See [scabies](/conditions/scabies).

## Treatment

1. **Soften and remove crusts** with warm water or a damp cloth, gently, once or twice a day.
2. **Apply the prescribed antibiotic ointment** to the cleaned sores for the days advised, often five to seven.
3. **Use antibiotic tablets** when sores are widespread, when ointment is not working or in bullous impetigo.
4. **Do not use steroid creams** on infected skin.
5. **Treat the cause,** such as eczema or scabies.

## How Long Is It Contagious?

Until sores have dried and crusted, or until about a day after starting antibiotics. Children can usually return to school once treatment has started and sores are covered or healing, but school rules vary.

## Protecting Others

- Wash hands often, especially after touching sores.
- Use separate towels, flannels and bedding for the child.
- Keep nails short and discourage scratching.
- Cover sores with a loose dressing.
- Wash clothing and bedding hot.

## Warning Signs

Fever, spreading redness, painful swelling, red streaks or very dark urine after impetigo need urgent medical care.

## Recurrent Impetigo

If it keeps returning, look for eczema, scabies, nose carriage of bacteria or poor skin barrier. Treating these prevents recurrence. See [eczema in babies](/blog/eczema-in-babies-first-year-skin-care-and-flare-plan).
