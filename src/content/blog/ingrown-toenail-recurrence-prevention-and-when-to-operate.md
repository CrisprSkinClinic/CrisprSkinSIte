An ingrown toenail is one of the most common minor problems we see, and many people try to deal with it themselves for a long time before they come in. Some of that self-care is helpful. Some of it makes things worse. And at a certain point, the honest advice is that the nail needs a definitive treatment. Here is how to tell the difference.

## What Causes It

An ingrown toenail occurs when the edge of the nail grows into the skin beside it. Common contributing factors include cutting nails too short or rounding the corners, tight or pointed footwear, repeated pressure or injury to the toe, naturally curved nails, and sweaty feet. It most often affects the big toe.

## What You Can Do at Home

If the problem is early and mild, with some tenderness and slight redness but no pus or severe pain, these measures can help.

**Cut nails properly.** Trim straight across, leaving the corners visible, and do not cut too short. Do not round the edges.

**Choose footwear carefully.** Wear shoes with a roomy toe box, and avoid tight or pointed styles while it settles.

**Keep it clean and dry.** Gentle washing and thorough drying help, and some people find warm soaks comfortable.

**Avoid pressure.** Protect the toe from knocks and pressure while it heals.

## What Not to Do

**Do not dig at the nail edge.** Trying to cut or pick out the ingrown corner yourself often leaves a sharp spike behind that digs in again, and can introduce infection.

**Do not ignore signs of infection.** Increasing pain, swelling, pus, or spreading redness need prompt attention.

**Do not rely on home remedies for a recurrent or severe problem.** They may ease symptoms for a while but do not change the cause.

## Signs It Needs Medical Attention

See us promptly if you have significant pain, swelling, pus or discharge, overgrown tender tissue along the nail edge, a fever, or spreading redness. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or reduced sensation in the feet should seek help early, even for minor problems.

## When a Procedure Makes Sense

A definitive procedure is usually considered when the problem is persistent, moderately to severely painful, or recurrent. It is also considered if home care has not worked, if the toe has repeatedly become infected, or if simply removing the edge in the past was followed by regrowth. ## Why Waiting Too Long Can Backfire

Repeated inflammation can leave chronic overgrown tissue and make the toe more prone to infection. Most patients who wait through many flares wish they had dealt with it earlier. That said, not everyone needs a procedure, and we are happy to advise on conservative options first.

## Special Situations

**People with diabetes or poor circulation.** Even a minor ingrown toenail can become a serious problem if sensation is reduced or healing is slow. Do not attempt self-treatment, and have the toe examined early.

**Teenagers and children.** Ingrown nails are common in adolescents, often from tight shoes or poor trimming. Conservative care works for many, and a procedure is reserved for persistent cases.

**Pregnancy and older age.** Swollen feet, changes in footwear, and difficulty reaching the toes can all contribute, so simple measures such as roomier shoes and help with nail care matter.

**Athletes.** Repeated pressure from running or football boots is a common cause. Properly fitted footwear and careful trimming usually help.

## Related Conditions

Recurring swelling around a nail can also be [paronychia](/conditions/paronychia), an inflammation of the nail fold, or the nail may be affected by [a fungal infection](/conditions/onychomycosis). An examination tells them apart, since treatment differs.

Good nail care solves many early cases. If yours is recurrent, painful, or infected, see [recurrent ingrown toenails](/conditions/recurrent-ingrown-toenail) and consider an assessment. A short procedure can end a long-running problem.
