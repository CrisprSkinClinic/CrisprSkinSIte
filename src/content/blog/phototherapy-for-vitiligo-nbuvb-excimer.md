Phototherapy is among the best-established and most effective treatments for vitiligo, especially when patches are widespread or when creams alone have not been enough. It uses carefully controlled ultraviolet light, delivered in a clinic, to calm the immune activity in the skin and stimulate pigment cells to return.

## How Light Helps

Ultraviolet light has two useful effects in vitiligo. It reduces the immune activity that destroys pigment cells, and it stimulates the surviving melanocytes, particularly those in hair follicles, to multiply and move into the depigmented skin. That is why repigmentation commonly begins as small pigmented dots around hair follicles, which gradually enlarge and join together.

## Narrowband UVB

Narrowband UVB uses a specific, narrow band of ultraviolet B wavelength and is the usual first-line light treatment for extensive vitiligo. It is generally considered safer and more effective than older broad-spectrum options.

**What a course involves.** Sessions are short, often lasting seconds to a few minutes, and are typically given two to three times a week. The dose is started low and increased gradually according to how the skin responds, which helps prevent burning. You wear eye protection, and treated areas are exposed while sensitive areas such as the genitals are covered. Treatment is usually continued for many months, because repigmentation is slow.

**What to expect.** Early signs of response are often seen after a few months, and a fair trial usually means at least three to six months of regular sessions. Response differs by body area: the face and neck do best, while hands and feet respond more slowly. Consistency matters a great deal, so missing many sessions lowers the chance of a good result.

**Side effects.** The most common are redness, dryness, and itching, and occasionally a sunburn-like reaction, which dose adjustment helps avoid. Normal skin around the patches may tan, which can make the contrast more noticeable early on but often becomes less obvious as patches repigment. Long-term, a main concern with any UV treatment is cumulative exposure, which is why total treatment duration and dose are monitored.

## Excimer Light and Laser

The excimer system delivers a high-intensity beam of UVB light to a small, targeted area. Because it treats only the patch and spares the surrounding skin, it is useful for limited or localised vitiligo, and for individual stubborn patches. It can deliver a higher dose to the patch than whole-body light, and some patients respond faster. The trade-off is that it is practical only for small areas, since each patch must be treated individually.

## PUVA

PUVA combines a light-sensitising medicine, taken by mouth or applied to the skin, with UVA light. It was widely used in the past, but it has largely been replaced by narrowband UVB, which is simpler, better tolerated, and does not require the sensitising medicine. PUVA may still have a role in selected cases, and when used, it needs careful eye protection and monitoring.

## Home Light Units

Some patients need to attend regularly for months, which is a real practical burden. Home phototherapy units exist, but they need careful selection, training, and supervision, since overdosing can cause burns and underdosing wastes effort. Whether this is appropriate for you is something to discuss with your doctor.

## Who Benefits Most

Phototherapy is generally recommended for vitiligo covering a larger area, for [active disease](/blog/stable-or-active-vitiligo-why-it-matters), and for patches that have not responded to creams. It is also used before and after [surgery](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-options-explained), since it can help stabilise disease and improve repigmentation of grafted areas.

## Combining Phototherapy With Other Treatment

Many patients use phototherapy with a [topical cream](/blog/vitiligo-topical-treatments-guide), which can improve results, especially on resistant patches. We choose the combination depending on your skin and sites involved.

## Safety and Practical Points

Tell your doctor about any medicines you take, including herbal products, because some can make your skin sensitive to light. Report any burning, blistering, or unusual symptoms. Wear sunscreen on exposed skin outside treatment, since sunburn on depigmented skin can be significant.

Phototherapy is a commitment of time and patience, but for many people with extensive vitiligo, it is the most effective option. If you are considering it, an assessment will help us decide whether it suits your pattern and how to set realistic goals for response.
