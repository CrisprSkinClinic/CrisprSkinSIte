Rosacea responds well to treatment, and the right choice depends on whether your main problem is redness, bumps or flushing.

## Creams and Gels

- **Azelaic acid:** reduces bumps and redness, and fades marks. Useful in brown skin. Can sting at first.
- **Metronidazole:** a long-established anti-inflammatory cream or gel for bumps.
- **Ivermectin cream:** reduces bumps and targets skin mites, effective for many.
- **Brimonidine or oxymetazoline gel:** temporarily narrows blood vessels to reduce persistent redness for several hours, used sparingly, with the possibility of rebound.

Most take four to eight weeks to show benefit, and are used long term to maintain control.

## Tablets

- **Low-dose doxycycline:** used for its anti-inflammatory effect, not as an antibiotic, over weeks to months, for moderate to severe bumps and for eye involvement.
- **Other antibiotics** are sometimes used in short courses.
- **Low-dose isotretinoin** for severe, resistant or thickened-skin rosacea, under specialist supervision.
- **Propranolol or similar** for troublesome flushing in selected patients.

Tablets need a medical assessment, since they have side effects and cannot be used in all patients, such as in pregnancy.

## Light and Laser

- **Vascular lasers and some light devices** shrink visible vessels and reduce persistent redness. Several sessions are needed. In brown skin, settings are chosen carefully.
- They treat redness but do not cure the underlying disease, so medicines and routine are still needed.

## Combining Treatments

A common plan is a cream for bumps, a gentle routine and sun protection, with tablets for a flare and laser for persistent redness.

## What to Avoid

Steroid creams on the face, scrubs, and treatments for acne unless advised, since many irritate rosacea.

## How Long Will It Take?

Improvement within two to three months, and control with maintenance. Stopping treatment too early often leads to relapse. Review your plan regularly.
