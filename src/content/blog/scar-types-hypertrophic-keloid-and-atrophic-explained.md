Not all scars are the same, and treating them as if they were is the commonest reason for disappointing results. Knowing your scar type is the first step.

## How Scars Form

When the skin is cut, burned or inflamed deeply enough to damage the lower layer (dermis), the body repairs it with collagen. The amount, direction and balance of that collagen decide how the scar looks. Too little leaves a dip, too much leaves a bump, and tension leaves a wide line.

## 1. Flat Scars

Fine, flat scars that start pink or red and fade over six to twelve months to a pale line. These are the normal result of most cuts and surgery. They rarely need treatment, though position and width can bother some people.

## 2. Hypertrophic Scars

Raised, red, firm scars that stay within the boundary of the original wound. They appear within weeks, often on areas of tension such as the chest, shoulders and joints, and are itchy or tender. Many flatten and fade over a year or two, and treatment can speed this up.

## 3. Keloids

Raised scars that grow beyond the original wound, like a claw, and may keep growing. They are firm, shiny, pink to dark brown, and may itch or ache. They are more frequent in darker skin, on the earlobes, chest, shoulders and upper back, and in people with a family history. They rarely improve alone, and often return after surgery. See [keloid treatment](/blog/keloid-treatment-what-works-and-why-keloids-return) and [keloids on the earlobe](/blog/keloids-on-the-earlobe-what-to-know).

## 4. Atrophic (Sunken) Scars

Depressed scars from loss of tissue, most often after acne or chickenpox. Icepick, boxcar and rolling scars are the patterns. See [acne scar treatments by type](/blog/acne-scar-treatments-by-scar-type-compared).

## 5. Stretch Marks (Striae)

Linear marks from rapid stretching of the skin in pregnancy, growth spurts, weight change and some medicines. They start red or purple, then fade to white and slightly depressed. See [stretch marks: what treatment can do](/blog/stretch-marks-treatment-what-is-realistic).

## 6. Contracture Scars

Tight scars after burns that pull on the skin and limit movement. They need specialist surgical assessment.

## 7. Marks That Are Not Scars

Flat red or brown patches after spots or wounds are pigment or blood-vessel changes, not scars, and often fade with time and sun protection. See [red and brown marks after acne](/blog/red-and-brown-marks-after-acne-vs-true-scars).

## Why the Type Matters

A keloid should not simply be cut out. A sunken scar will not improve with steroid injections. A wide surgical scar needs revision, not just creams. Treatment must match the scar.

## Who Is More Likely to Scar Badly?

People with darker skin, a family history of keloids, teenagers and young adults, wounds on the chest, shoulders and earlobes, and wounds under tension or that became infected.

See a dermatologist for a scar that is raised, itchy, growing, painful, wide, or that affects movement. Early treatment gives the best result. See our [scar revision service](/services/scar-revision).
