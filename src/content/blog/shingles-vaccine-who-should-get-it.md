Shingles is a painful blistering rash caused by the chickenpox virus, which stays dormant in nerve cells after childhood chickenpox. Later in life, or when immunity dips, it can wake up. The vaccine is the most effective way to prevent it.

## Who Should Take It

The recombinant (non-live) shingles vaccine is recommended for adults from 50 years. It is also advised for adults from 18 years who have weakened immunity, or who are about to start medicines such as [biologics](/blog/biologics-in-dermatology-explained), JAK inhibitors, methotrexate or long-term steroids.

## How It Is Given

It is a two-dose course. For most adults the second dose follows two to six months after the first. When immunity is weakened, the interval can be shortened to one to two months so protection is in place sooner.

## If You Have Already Had Shingles

Take it anyway once the rash has fully healed. Shingles can return, and the vaccine lowers that risk.

## What to Expect Afterwards

A sore arm, tiredness, aches or a mild fever for a day or two are common and show that the vaccine is working. Serious reactions are rare. Seek care for swelling of the face or lips, or difficulty breathing.

## Why It Is Worth It

The main danger of shingles is not the rash but the nerve pain that can follow and last for months, which is hard to treat. Prevention is far easier. Read [what to do in the first 72 hours](/blog/shingles-symptoms-treatment-first-72-hours) if you ever develop the rash.

## Common Questions

**Is the shingles vaccine a live vaccine?** The recombinant vaccine we use is not live. It contains a single viral protein with a booster ingredient (an adjuvant), so it cannot cause shingles or chickenpox. This is why it is also considered for people with weakened immunity, for whom live vaccines are usually avoided.

**Do I need it if I never had chickenpox?** Almost every adult in India has had the chickenpox virus, often without a clear illness in childhood, so the virus is likely dormant in your nerves. Your doctor may check for immunity if there is doubt. If you are not immune, chickenpox vaccination, not shingles vaccination, is the question.

**How long does protection last?** In studies, protection stayed high for at least several years after the two doses, and follow-up continues. Booster advice may change as longer data become available.

**Can I take it with other vaccines?** Usually yes. Your doctor can advise on timing with influenza, pneumococcal or COVID-19 vaccines.

**Is it safe if I am on a biologic or steroids?** The recombinant vaccine is generally considered suitable for people on immunosuppressants, and it is best given before starting treatment where possible. We plan this with you. See our [pre-treatment vaccine checklist](/blog/vaccines-before-immunosuppressants-biologics-checklist).

## Who Should Wait or Check First

You should postpone the vaccine if you have a moderate or severe illness with fever, and until you recover from an active shingles rash. If you have ever had a severe allergic reaction to a vaccine ingredient, tell your doctor. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are situations where we usually advise waiting unless there is a strong reason, and take individual advice.

## What a Visit Looks Like

We ask about past chickenpox or shingles, other illnesses, and medicines. We explain what to expect, give the first injection in the upper arm, and ask you to wait for a short observation period. You leave with a date for the second dose and a note of what is normal over the next two days: a sore arm, fatigue, aches. Most people go back to normal activity the same day.

## Shingles in Numbers

Roughly one in three people will get shingles in their lifetime, and the risk climbs steeply after the age of fifty. About one in five of those who get it have nerve pain that lingers for months. Those figures are why vaccination is recommended for adults from fifty and for people with weakened immunity from eighteen. Prevention is far easier than treating long-lasting nerve pain.
