Some patients notice that after a transplant not only the transplanted hair sheds, but also some of the existing hair nearby. Seeing hair you thought was safe fall out can be genuinely distressing. This is called shock loss, and in most cases it is temporary. Understanding what it is, who gets it, and what to expect can take much of the worry out of it.

## What Shock Loss Is

Shock loss is the temporary shedding of existing, native hair in or around the area that was operated on. It is different from the planned shedding of the transplanted hair shafts themselves, which is [expected in the first few weeks](/blog/hair-transplant-timeline-month-by-month). In shock loss, hairs that were already growing in your scalp enter a resting phase and fall out, usually within the first few weeks after surgery.

## Why It Happens

The exact mechanism is not fully understood, but the leading explanation is that the stress of the procedure on the skin, including the local anaesthetic, the small incisions made to place grafts, and local inflammation, pushes some existing hairs out of their growth phase early and into a resting phase. Reduced blood supply in the area for a short time may also play a part. The follicles are not destroyed. They are resting, and most re-enter the growth phase after a few months.

## Who Is More Likely to Experience It

Shock loss is more likely when grafts are placed among existing native hairs, in areas of thinning rather than complete baldness. Patients with early, diffuse thinning are therefore more likely to notice it than those who are bald in the recipient area, because there is more native hair to be affected. It can also occur in the donor area in some cases, though this is less common.

This is one reason careful planning matters when transplanting into thinning areas. Sometimes a plan includes protecting existing hair as far as possible, or advising on medical treatment to support native hair before and after surgery.

## What It Looks Like

Patients typically notice increased shedding of thin native hairs in the weeks after surgery, along with the shedding of transplanted hairs. The area may look thinner than before the procedure for a while. Because this coincides with the quiet phase of transplanted hair, it can seem as though the procedure has made things worse. It has not, in most cases.

## How Long It Lasts

Most native hairs affected by shock loss begin to regrow within about three to four months, though timing varies, and full recovery can take up to around six to nine months. Recovery is not always complete in every case, but most patients regain most or all of the shed hair. Individual response varies, which is why we monitor progress at follow-up visits.

## What Helps

There is no proven way to prevent shock loss entirely, but several measures help support recovery. Follow your aftercare instructions closely. Maintain any [medical treatment](/blog/medical-therapy-with-hair-transplant) advised to protect native hair, since medicines that support existing hair may help limit loss and aid regrowth. Eat a balanced diet, avoid smoking, and manage stress where possible. Avoid unproven products or treatments that promise to prevent shock loss, as they add expense without reliable benefit.

## When to Seek Advice

Contact us if shedding comes with pain, spreading redness, pus, fever, or crusting, which could suggest infection. Also let us know if the pattern is very different from what we described at your visit, if shedding seems extreme, or if there is no sign of regrowth after the expected time. Occasionally, other conditions such as an underlying scalp problem or nutritional deficiency contribute and need to be checked.

## Why Planning Matters

We take shock loss into account when planning a transplant. Where there is substantial native hair, we discuss the risk openly, including the possibility that some existing hair may shed for a time. We consider whether medical treatment should come first, how to place grafts to minimise disturbance, and how to set expectations for the first months.

## Reassurance

In most cases shock loss resolves without treatment. Understanding it in advance makes the experience far less stressful, and the temporary thinning does not reflect the final result. If you are worried about something you see, come in and we will take a look rather than leaving you to wonder.
