The supplement shelf is full of promises. Here is a reality check.

## Supported When a Deficiency Exists

Iron, vitamin D, vitamin B12 and zinc help when levels are truly low, and your doctor can test for them. In those cases, correcting them can reduce hair shedding and improve overall health.

## Limited or Unproven

- **Biotin:** helps in rare biotin deficiency. In other people, there is little evidence for hair or nail growth. High doses can interfere with thyroid and heart blood tests, so tell your doctor if you take it.
- **Collagen:** some small studies suggest modest improvement in skin hydration, but results are mixed. See [collagen supplements](/blog/collagen-supplements-what-evidence-says).
- **Antioxidants and "glow" blends:** the evidence of benefit on skin appearance is weak.
- **Omega-3:** may help general health, but is not a skin treatment.

## Risks

High doses of vitamin A, selenium and some herbal products can cause hair loss, liver injury or other harm. More is not better.

## Better Spending

Daily sunscreen, a good moisturiser, treatment of acne or pigmentation, and medicines for [pattern hair loss](/services/hair-loss-medical-treatment) do more than most supplements.

## If You Want to Take One

Choose one with a clear reason, check the dose, and review after three months. Tell your doctor about everything you take.

## Why the Market Is So Crowded

Supplements are sold as foods, not medicines, so they are held to lower standards of evidence and quality. Studies are often funded by manufacturers, are small, or use measures such as self-reported improvement. Labels may not match contents, and some products contain hidden ingredients or contaminants. This does not mean all supplements are useless, but it does mean you should be a sceptical customer.

## Evidence at a Glance

- **Iron, vitamin D, B12, zinc:** useful when deficiency is confirmed by testing.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids:** support heart health, with minor or uncertain effects on skin.
- **Biotin:** ineffective for hair or nails in people with normal levels. Interferes with some blood tests.
- **Collagen peptides:** some trials show small improvements in skin hydration and elasticity over weeks. See [collagen supplements](/blog/collagen-supplements-what-evidence-says).
- **Vitamin C and E:** essential nutrients with antioxidant effects, but high-dose supplements are not shown to improve skin in people who eat enough.
- **Probiotics:** limited, strain-specific and inconsistent benefit.
- **Hair, skin and nail multivitamin blends:** most contain a basic mixture of vitamins at doses that may exceed need, with little evidence of benefit.
- **Herbal products (for example saw palmetto, ashwagandha):** limited data, with variable quality. Some can interact with medicines or affect the liver.

## Safety Concerns

- Vitamin A in excess causes hair loss and is harmful in pregnancy.
- Selenium over-dosing causes hair and nail loss.
- Iron overload damages the liver and heart.
- High-dose zinc impairs copper and can cause anaemia.
- Some "natural" products contain steroids or other drugs that are not listed.

## Questions to Ask Before Buying

1. What exactly is it for, and has a doctor found a deficiency?
2. What does the evidence say, in people like me?
3. Is the dose within safe limits?
4. Does it interact with my medicines?
5. Is it from a reputable manufacturer with batch testing?

Money spent on sunscreen, a moisturiser that suits your skin, treatment for acne or [pattern hair loss](/services/hair-loss-medical-treatment), and healthy meals will do more than most supplements.
