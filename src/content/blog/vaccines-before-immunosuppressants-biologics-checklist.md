Medicines that lower immunity treat psoriasis, eczema, hidradenitis and other skin disease very well. They also change how vaccines behave. A few weeks of planning before starting can prevent avoidable infections.

## Live and Non-Live Vaccines

Live vaccines are generally avoided once treatment begins. If one is needed, it is usually given at least four weeks beforehand. Non-live vaccines are safe at any time, but produce a better response when given at least two weeks before treatment. Urgent treatment should never be held up unnecessarily.

## What We Usually Review

- **Shingles (recombinant, non-live)** for adults, including those from 18 years with weakened immunity. See [who should take it](/blog/shingles-vaccine-who-should-get-it).
- **HPV**, if not already completed.
- **Hepatitis B**, with a blood test to check immunity. Read [more on hepatitis B](/blog/hepatitis-b-vaccination-before-skin-procedures).
- **Influenza and pneumococcal** vaccines, which your physician usually provides.
- **Chickenpox and measles immunity**, because live vaccines would need to be given early.
- **COVID-19 and tetanus boosters**, as advised.

## Also Part of Screening

## Why Immunosuppression Changes Vaccine Advice

Biologics, JAK inhibitors, methotrexate, azathioprine, ciclosporin and long-term steroids quieten parts of the immune system. This helps the skin, but it also means two things for vaccines. First, a live vaccine contains a weakened but living germ, and in a person with suppressed immunity it can, rarely, cause the infection it was meant to prevent. Second, non-live vaccines are safe but may produce a weaker response while you are on treatment, so protection is better when they are given first.

## A Practical Timeline

A reasonable plan, to be individualised:

1. **Four to six weeks before starting:** book a review. We check your records, blood tests and exposure history.
2. **At least four weeks before:** any live vaccines you need, such as measles-containing vaccines or chickenpox, if you are not immune.
3. **At least two weeks before:** non-live vaccines such as shingles, hepatitis B, pneumococcal, influenza, HPV and tetanus-containing boosters.
4. **After starting:** non-live vaccines continue as needed, with timing adjusted around medicines that blunt responses.

If your skin disease is severe and treatment cannot wait, we do not delay it for a vaccine. We vaccinate with non-live options as soon as possible, and plan the rest.

## What About Methotrexate and Biologics Specifically?

Methotrexate can lower the response to some vaccines, and for influenza vaccination your doctor may advise a short pause of methotrexate around the dose in some situations. Biologics differ in how much they affect vaccine response, and some need live vaccines to be avoided for a period after stopping. We check the specifics for your medicine.

## Blood Tests Often Done at the Same Time

Hepatitis B and C screening, a test for tuberculosis, and sometimes tests for chickenpox and measles antibodies. Our page on [biologics](/services/biologics) describes the full pre-treatment screening.

## Household Contacts

Family members can usually receive routine vaccines. Some live vaccines given to children, such as oral polio in the past or rotavirus, can shed virus briefly, and we advise simple hygiene afterwards. Ask us if a baby in the house is due a vaccine.

## Keep a Record

Bring all your vaccination certificates, or take photos of them. A single page listing the vaccines, dates and batch details makes planning much easier, and helps if you later see another specialist.
