Even with good treatment, some patches respond slowly or not at all. The hands, feet, lips, and areas over bony prominences are particularly stubborn, and long-standing patches with white hair often resist treatment. A good vitiligo plan includes options for these situations, and for patients who simply prefer not to wait for treatment to work. None of these is a failure of treatment. They are legitimate choices.

## Cosmetic Camouflage

Camouflage covers the patch with colour. Specially formulated waterproof cosmetics and body make-up can match skin tone and stay in place through the day, and some are designed to resist rubbing and sweat. Self-tanning products containing a colouring agent can also be used to darken depigmented skin, though the result can look uneven and fades as the skin surface renews. Some people use dyes or stains made for this purpose.

**Practical points.** Colour matching takes practice, and it helps to try a few products before settling on one. Test any new product on a small area first, since cosmetics can cause allergic reactions, and avoid products with harsh ingredients. Camouflage can be used alongside [medical treatment](/blog/vitiligo-treatment-roadmap-what-to-expect), and many patients combine the two. Ask us for guidance, since the right shade and formulation depend on your skin tone.

## Micropigmentation (Medical Tattooing)

Micropigmentation places pigment into the skin using a needle, similar to a tattoo but done with colours matched to your skin. It is sometimes used for small, stable patches, particularly on the lips, where other treatments are often disappointing, and sometimes on the areola or other limited areas.

**Limitations.** Matching colour is difficult, and the pigment can fade or change shade over time, so touch-ups may be needed. The tattoo does not stop the underlying disease, and injury to the skin can occasionally trigger new patches (the Koebner phenomenon) in people with active vitiligo, which is why this is considered only for [stable areas](/blog/stable-or-active-vitiligo-why-it-matters). Choose a practitioner with specific experience, and discuss it with your dermatologist first.

## Sun Protection

Depigmented skin has no melanin, so it burns easily, and burning can worsen vitiligo in some people. Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen on exposed patches is advised, along with shade and protective clothing when practical. Sunscreen also reduces tanning of the surrounding skin, which makes the contrast between patches and normal skin less pronounced.

## Depigmentation: Lightening the Remaining Skin

For people with very extensive vitiligo, where most of the body is already depigmented and repigmentation is not realistic, some choose to lighten the remaining pigmented skin so the tone becomes uniform. This is done with a medicated cream that permanently removes the remaining pigment cells over months.

**This is a serious decision.** It is permanent, it means lifelong strict sun protection because the skin will have no pigment, and it can take a year or more to complete. It can also cause skin irritation, and the visual change is not reversible. It is generally discussed only when a large majority of the skin is already affected, after other options have been considered, and after the patient has had time to reflect. Counselling is an important part of the process, and the decision should never be rushed or made under pressure.

## Why These Options Matter

Many people feel that unless a patch repigments, treatment has failed. We do not see it that way. The goal is a plan that gives you the best outcome you can reasonably achieve and a life you are comfortable with. For some people, that means chasing repigmentation. For others, it means covering patches and getting on with life. Both are valid.

## Emotional Wellbeing

Vitiligo can affect confidence, relationships, work, and mood, particularly when patches are visible. Some patients feel anxious in social situations, avoid certain clothing or activities, or experience low mood. This is common, and it deserves attention.

Talk to us openly about how you are coping. Counselling can help, and so can joining a support group, where you meet others who understand. Families, especially of children, benefit from guidance on how to talk about vitiligo and support the person affected. Looking after the person matters as much as treating the patches.
