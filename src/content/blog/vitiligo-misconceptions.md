Vitiligo is surrounded by more misinformation than almost any other common skin condition we treat. Much of it comes from old beliefs rather than current medical understanding, and it causes real harm: delayed treatment, unnecessary shame, and in some families, strained relationships and marriage prospects. It is worth addressing the most common misconceptions directly.

## "It Is Contagious"

Vitiligo cannot be spread by touch, shared food, shared clothing, or any other contact. It is an autoimmune condition, meaning the body's own immune system mistakenly attacks the pigment-producing cells (melanocytes) in the skin. There is no germ involved, so there is nothing to catch. Children with vitiligo can attend school and play normally, and adults can live, work, and share a home without any risk to others.

## "It Is Caused by Diet, Such as Eating Fish With Milk"

This belief is widespread, but there is no scientific basis for it. No food combination has been shown to cause vitiligo, and no diet reliably prevents or reverses it. Cutting out foods usually just adds stress and can lead to nutritional gaps. A normal, balanced diet is appropriate. If blood tests show a deficiency, such as vitamin D or B12, correcting it is sensible for general health, but that is different from claiming it treats vitiligo.

## "It Is the Same as Leprosy or a Fungal Infection"

Vitiligo is neither. Leprosy is a bacterial infection with numbness and other nerve signs, while a fungal infection such as pityriasis versicolor causes scaly patches that respond to antifungal treatment. Vitiligo patches are typically smooth, sharply defined, and completely depigmented, and are not scaly or numb. Because other conditions can look similar, an examination, often with a Wood's lamp, is worth doing before any treatment begins.

## "It Is Caused by Poor Hygiene, a Curse, or Something You Did"

Nothing a person does or fails to do causes vitiligo. It has a genetic component, since it runs in some families and is more common in people with other autoimmune conditions such as thyroid disease. In many people there is no identifiable trigger at all. Blame has no place in this condition.

## "There Is No Treatment"

This is the opposite myth, and equally unhelpful. Vitiligo is manageable, and a range of treatments can meaningfully restore pigment in many patients. These include [creams](/blog/vitiligo-topical-treatments-guide), light treatment ([phototherapy](/blog/phototherapy-for-vitiligo-nbuvb-excimer)), short courses of oral medicine in rapidly spreading disease, and [surgical repigmentation](/conditions/vitiligo-surgical-treatment) for stable patches. Which of them suits you depends on the type of vitiligo, how much skin is involved, and whether the disease is currently [active or settled](/conditions/stable-vs-active-vitiligo).

## "There Is a Guaranteed Cure"

We think patients deserve honesty here. Treatment can restore pigment in many cases, especially with early and consistent treatment, but there is no guaranteed cure, and results vary. Advertisements and products that promise to cure vitiligo completely should be treated with caution. Even when patches repigment well, vitiligo can recur, so follow-up matters.

## "It Always Spreads Everywhere"

The course genuinely varies. Some people have a few small, stable patches for years. Others have more extensive or actively spreading depigmentation. A form called [segmental vitiligo](/conditions/segmental-vitiligo), which affects one side or area of the body, usually spreads for a limited period and then settles. We cannot predict exactly what will happen at a first visit, which is why ongoing assessment, rather than a single guess, is part of good care.

## "Sun Exposure Will Cure It"

Controlled light treatment under medical supervision can help, but unsupervised sun exposure is not the same thing. Depigmented skin has no protection against sunburn, and burning can make vitiligo worse in some people. Daily sunscreen on exposed patches is advised.

## "It Is Only a Cosmetic Problem"

Vitiligo does not usually cause physical symptoms, but the emotional effect is real. Many patients experience reduced confidence, anxiety, or low mood, particularly when patches are visible. This deserves to be taken seriously, and support, camouflage options, and counselling are legitimate parts of care.

## What Actually Helps

Response varies by body area, patch age, and disease activity. The face and neck generally respond best, while the hands and feet respond least reliably. Treatment is usually continued for months before a fair judgement can be made, so patience and regular review matter. For patients who want it, cosmetic camouflage is a perfectly reasonable option alongside medical treatment, not a sign that treatment has failed.

If you or someone in your family has new pale patches, the most useful step is an accurate diagnosis. From there, we can discuss which options fit your pattern, set realistic goals, and build a plan, without false promises and without judgement.
