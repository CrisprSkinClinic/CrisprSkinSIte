Surgical treatment for vitiligo works by moving pigment-producing cells from healthy skin into a depigmented patch. It can give excellent results in carefully chosen patients, but it is not for everyone, and it is only appropriate once the disease is stable. This article explains who qualifies, how the main techniques differ, and what to expect.

## Who Is a Candidate

The key requirement is stability. Patches should generally have shown no new spread and no enlargement for a sustained period, commonly around six to twelve months or more, and without new patches appearing at sites of injury or friction. Surgery on active vitiligo risks the disease appearing in the grafted skin or the donor site, undoing the result.

Surgery is typically considered for patches that have stayed stable but have not repigmented adequately with creams or light treatment. It is often a very good option for segmental vitiligo, which tends to respond poorly to medical therapy but to stabilise on its own. It is also used for small, localised patches in visible areas where the cosmetic benefit is high.

Patients with a tendency to form keloids or thick scars, those with unrealistic expectations, and those whose disease is still active are generally not good candidates. A test graft on a small area is sometimes done first to see how your skin responds, including whether it forms scars or develops pigment mismatch.

## The Main Techniques

**Punch grafting.** Small, round pieces of skin are taken from a pigmented donor site, usually the thigh or buttock, and placed into tiny holes made in the patch. This suits small, localised patches. Possible downsides include a cobblestone appearance, colour mismatch, and scarring at the donor site.

**Suction blister grafting.** A suction device raises blisters on the donor skin, and the thin roof of each blister, which contains pigment cells, is placed on the prepared patch. Because only the top layer is taken, donor-site scarring is minimal and the cosmetic result tends to be smooth. It is a commonly used option for moderate-sized patches.

**Non-cultured cell suspension transfer.** A small sample of donor skin is treated to separate its cells, and the resulting suspension of pigment cells and skin cells is applied to the prepared patch. A small donor area can treat a much larger patch, which makes it useful for larger areas. The technique requires specialised preparation and experience.

**Hair follicle transplantation.** Follicles carrying pigment cells are taken from the scalp or another hair-bearing area and implanted in the patch. This can be useful for segmental vitiligo and for hair-bearing sites, as follicles serve as a reservoir of pigment cells.

**Thin split-thickness skin grafts.** A thin layer of donor skin is placed on the patch. It can cover larger areas, but carries a higher risk of colour mismatch, uneven texture, and donor-site problems, so it is used selectively.

## How the Technique Is Chosen

The decision depends on the size, site, and type of patch, how much donor skin is available, the patient's skin type, and the experience of the surgeon. Some sites, such as lips, fingertips, and the backs of joints, are harder to treat than others, and results can be less predictable.

## What Recovery Looks Like

The treated and donor areas are covered with dressings and need protection from friction and wetting for a period. Healing takes several weeks, and pigment typically begins to appear over the following weeks to months. Many centres combine surgery with light treatment or sun exposure afterward, since this can help the transferred cells spread and improve colour match.

## Risks and Limitations

Not every graft takes, and results are rarely perfect. Possible problems include mismatch in colour, either lighter or darker than surrounding skin, a cobblestone or uneven surface, scarring, infection, and failure of the graft. Occasionally vitiligo can appear in the donor area or in new sites if the disease was not truly stable. Repigmentation may also be partial, and further sessions are sometimes needed.
