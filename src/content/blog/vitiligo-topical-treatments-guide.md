For limited vitiligo, creams are usually where treatment begins. They are not all the same, and the right choice depends on where the patches are, how large they are, and whether the disease is active. Using the wrong cream in the wrong place is one of the commonest reasons treatment disappoints, or causes side effects.

## What Topical Treatment Is Trying to Do

In vitiligo, the immune system attacks pigment-producing cells. Topical treatments aim to reduce that local immune activity so that surviving pigment cells, mainly those sitting deep in hair follicles, can multiply and migrate back out into the patch. This is why repigmentation often appears first as tiny pigmented dots around hair follicles before merging into larger areas.

## Topical Corticosteroids

Steroid creams reduce inflammation and the local immune attack, and they are effective for limited patches, especially when started early. Their strength varies, from mild to very potent, and the strength chosen depends on the body site.

The main concern is side effects with prolonged use. These can include thinning of the skin, visible blood vessels, stretch marks, and acne-like eruptions, and the risk is highest on the face, neck, armpits, groin, and other folds, where skin is thin and absorbs more. For this reason, steroids are generally used in short, supervised courses, often with planned breaks, rather than continuously. A common approach is to use a potent steroid for a limited period on trunk and limbs, and to avoid potent steroids on the face except briefly under close supervision.

## Calcineurin Inhibitors: Tacrolimus and Pimecrolimus

These creams act on the same immune process but through a different mechanism, and they do not cause skin thinning. That makes them especially useful on the face, neck, and skin folds, and suitable for longer-term use. Many doctors treat them as the preferred option for facial vitiligo.

They can cause burning, stinging, or flushing in the first days to weeks, which usually settles as the skin adjusts. Sun protection is advisable on treated areas. They are often applied twice daily, and in studies they have been used for many months. In some cases, applying tacrolimus along with sun exposure or light treatment improves response, though this should be guided by your doctor.

## Vitamin D Analogues

Creams containing vitamin D derivatives are sometimes used, mainly alongside other treatment. On their own they are generally less effective than steroids or tacrolimus, but they may add benefit in combination and can be useful when steroids need to be limited.

## Newer Topical Agents

[Topical JAK inhibitor creams](/blog/vitiligo-newer-treatment-developments), which block signalling inside immune cells, have shown repigmentation in clinical studies, with the best results on the face. Approval and availability differ between countries, and cost may be a factor. We will tell you honestly what is accessible and what the evidence supports, rather than recommending a product because it is new.

## Where Creams Work Best and Where They Struggle

Response differs across the body. The face and neck usually respond best, followed by the trunk and upper arms. Hands, fingers, feet, and areas over bony prominences such as knuckles, elbows, and knees respond the least reliably. Patches that have been present for many years also respond more slowly than recent ones. A patch with white hair in it, which means the pigment reserve in the follicle has also been lost, tends to respond poorly to creams alone.

## How Long to Persist

Repigmentation is slow. Treatment is usually continued for several months before a fair judgement of response can be made, and many patients need six months or longer. We review progress at regular intervals, often with photographs, and if a patch shows no response after an adequate trial, we change the plan rather than continuing indefinitely.

## Combining Creams With Other Treatment

Creams are frequently combined with light treatment, because the combination can work better than either alone, particularly for stubborn patches. For extensive vitiligo, topical treatment alone is usually not enough, and [phototherapy](/blog/phototherapy-for-vitiligo-nbuvb-excimer) is generally added.

## Practical Tips

Apply the cream as directed, in a thin layer only to the affected skin, and avoid applying steroids to normal skin around the patch. Take photographs under the same lighting every few weeks to track progress, since gradual change is hard to notice day to day. Use sunscreen on treated areas, and tell your doctor about any thinning, redness, or persistent burning.

If you have new or spreading patches, avoid self-treating with leftover or borrowed creams. A proper assessment first, including whether your vitiligo is [active or stable](/blog/stable-or-active-vitiligo-why-it-matters), helps us choose creams that suit your skin and your pattern.
