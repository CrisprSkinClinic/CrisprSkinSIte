Vitiligo treatment is a process, not a single decision. Many people arrive with a long list of questions, and it helps to see how the pieces fit together before getting into the details of each option. Here is how care typically unfolds, from the first visit onwards.

## Step 1: Diagnosis and Classification

The first visit focuses on confirming the diagnosis and understanding your particular pattern. We take a history covering when patches appeared, how they have changed, any family history of vitiligo or autoimmune conditions, and anything that preceded the onset such as illness, stress, injury, or sunburn. We examine the skin, often using a Wood's lamp, which makes depigmented areas easier to see and reveals patches that are hard to detect in normal light.

We then classify your vitiligo. Is it non-segmental, the more common type, or segmental, confined to one area? How much of the body is involved? Are there patches on the hands, feet, lips, or genitals, which are harder to treat? Are there white hairs within the patches? We may also screen for associated conditions, particularly thyroid disease, which occurs somewhat more often alongside vitiligo.

## Step 2: Is It Active or Stable?

Establishing whether the disease is spreading or settled is central, because it determines the treatment order. We use your history, examination, and photographs, and may reassess at later visits. See our article on [stable versus active vitiligo](/blog/stable-or-active-vitiligo-why-it-matters) for more detail.

## Step 3: Calm the Disease if It Is Active

For active, spreading vitiligo, the first aim is to halt progression. Options include narrowband UVB phototherapy, topical treatment, and, in rapidly progressing cases, a short, supervised course of oral medicine to help stop spread, with careful monitoring because of possible side effects. Protecting skin from sunburn and injury is also part of this phase.

## Step 4: Restore Pigment

Once the disease is calmer, treatment focuses on repigmentation. For limited patches, [topical treatment](/blog/vitiligo-topical-treatments-guide) is often the start: steroid creams in short supervised courses for the body, and tacrolimus-type creams for the face, neck, and folds. For more extensive vitiligo, [narrowband UVB phototherapy](/blog/phototherapy-for-vitiligo-nbuvb-excimer) is the established first-line option. Excimer light can target individual stubborn patches. Treatments are often combined for better results.

This phase takes time. Repigmentation usually starts as small pigmented dots around hair follicles after a few months, and a fair trial typically needs six months or longer. We review at regular intervals, with photographs, and adjust the plan if a patch is not responding.

## Step 5: Consider Surgery for Resistant, Stable Patches

For patches that have been stable but have not repigmented with medical treatment, [surgical repigmentation](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-options-explained) may be considered. Techniques include punch grafting, suction blister grafting, cell suspension transfer, and hair follicle transplantation, and the choice depends on the size and site of the patch. Segmental vitiligo is often a particularly good candidate. This step is only appropriate when the disease has been stable for a sustained period.

## Step 6: Supportive Care and Long-Term Follow-Up

Alongside medical treatment, we offer supportive options: daily sun protection, cosmetic camouflage, and, in selected cases, medical tattooing for stable patches. For very extensive vitiligo, where repigmentation is not realistic, we [discuss lightening the remaining skin](/blog/vitiligo-camouflage-micropigmentation-and-depigmentation), but only after other options and careful reflection.

Because vitiligo can recur, follow-up continues after a good response. We recommend monitoring for new patches, and returning early if you notice changes, since early treatment generally works better.

## Looking After the Whole Person

Vitiligo affects more than skin. Many patients experience reduced confidence, anxiety, or low mood, particularly when patches are visible. We ask about this and can suggest counselling or support groups. For children, we involve parents in guidance on talking about vitiligo.

## What We Mean by Realistic Expectations

Response varies by body area, how long the patch has been present, and how active the disease is. The face and neck respond best, and hands and feet least reliably. Some patches repigment fully, some partly, and some not at all. We tell you what we expect for your pattern, and we will not promise outcomes we cannot reliably deliver.

If you are starting out, bring photographs of when your patches first appeared if you have them, a list of medicines you take, and your questions. A clear plan matched to your pattern is the aim of the first visit.
