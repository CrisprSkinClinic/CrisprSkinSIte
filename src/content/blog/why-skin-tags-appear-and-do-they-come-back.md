Skin tags are small, soft growths that hang from the skin. Almost half of adults have at least one. They are harmless, and understanding why they appear helps you prevent new ones.

## What a Skin Tag Is

A skin tag (acrochordon) is a small piece of loose skin and fibrous tissue on a thin stalk, usually the colour of your skin or slightly darker. It ranges from a pinhead to a few millimetres or, rarely, bigger. It is not contagious, and it does not turn into cancer.

## Where They Grow

Mostly in places where skin rubs against skin or clothing: the neck, armpits, groin, eyelids, under the breasts and the upper chest.

## Why They Appear

- **Friction.** Skin folds and clothing rub the skin, and this is the best-known trigger.
- **Insulin resistance and weight gain.** Tags are more common in people who are overweight, have prediabetes or diabetes, and in those with PCOS. High insulin levels encourage the skin cells to grow.
- **Pregnancy,** when hormones and weight change.
- **Family history.** Tags run in families.
- **Age.** They become more common through adult life.
- **Certain conditions,** such as acanthosis nigricans, which often appears with tags.

## Do They Signal a Health Problem?

A few tags on their own are normal. Many tags, a sudden crop of new ones, or tags with a dark, velvety neck can point to insulin resistance, so a blood sugar test is worthwhile. ## Will They Come Back After Removal?

A removed skin tag does not grow back. But the same causes remain, so new tags can appear nearby or elsewhere. People with ongoing weight gain or insulin resistance tend to get more.

## Lowering the Chance of New Tags

- Keep weight in a healthy range, and treat prediabetes or diabetes.
- Reduce friction: loose, soft clothing and jewellery that does not rub the neck.
- Keep skin folds clean and dry.
- Check blood sugar if tags are multiplying.

## When Is It Not a Skin Tag?

A growth that is dark, hard, irregular, bleeding, painful or growing quickly may be something else, such as a mole, wart or another lesion. See a dermatologist before removal. See [mole removal](/services/mole-removal).
