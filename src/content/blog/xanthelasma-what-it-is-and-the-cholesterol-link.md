Many people notice soft, yellowish patches appearing near the inner corners of their eyelids and assume they are a cosmetic nuisance. They are usually harmless to the eye, but they are worth understanding, because for some people they offer a visible clue to what is happening in the blood.

## What Xanthelasma Is

Xanthelasma are flat or slightly raised yellowish plaques on or around the eyelids, most often near the inner corners of the upper lids, and often on both sides. They are made of cells in the skin that have taken up fat, giving the yellow colour. They are painless, do not affect vision, and are not cancerous.

## What Causes Them

The deposits form when fatty material collects in the skin, and several things can contribute.

**Raised cholesterol or other lipids.** In a substantial proportion of people with xanthelasma, blood lipid levels are abnormal. This includes high LDL cholesterol, raised triglycerides, or low HDL cholesterol, and in some people an inherited condition that causes very high cholesterol.

**Other medical conditions.** Diabetes, an underactive thyroid, and certain liver or kidney problems can affect blood fats and contribute.

**Normal lipids.** A good number of people with xanthelasma have normal cholesterol. Their patches are not explained by blood fats, and the reason is not fully understood. This is why testing, not assumption, matters.

**Age and sex.** They are more common in middle age and beyond, and in women.

## Why Check Your Cholesterol

High cholesterol rarely causes symptoms, yet it increases the long-term risk of heart attack and stroke. Xanthelasma can be an early visible sign, especially in younger adults or with a family history of early heart disease or high cholesterol. Finding and treating a lipid problem is far more important for your health than removing the patches.

## What We Check

We usually recommend a fasting lipid profile, which measures total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides, and sometimes blood sugar and thyroid tests. We ask about your family history, diet, activity, smoking, and medicines. If lipids are raised, treatment, often lifestyle changes and sometimes medicines, is arranged with your doctor or a specialist.

## Who Should Be Particularly Keen to Get Tested

Anyone with xanthelasma should have their lipids checked, but the case is especially strong if you are under about 40, if close relatives had high cholesterol or early heart disease, if you have diabetes, thyroid disease, or high blood pressure, or if you have other fatty deposits, such as lumps over the tendons of the hands or heels. In those situations, an inherited lipid disorder is more likely, and identifying it early lets treatment start before any damage occurs.

## Does Treating Cholesterol Make Patches Disappear?

Not reliably. Lowering cholesterol may stop patches from growing or recurring, and a few may soften, but established patches usually need to be removed if you want them gone. Creams and home remedies do not dissolve them, and we would caution against products that promise to.

## When It Could Be Something Else

Not every eyelid bump is xanthelasma. Cysts, skin tags, warts, and rarely skin cancers can look similar. A lesion that is hard, ulcerated, bleeding, rapidly growing, or not yellow needs an examination. See [xanthelasma](/conditions/xanthelasma) for how we tell them apart.

## The Bottom Line

Xanthelasma are usually harmless to the eye, but they are a good reason to check your cholesterol. If you have noticed yellow patches on your eyelids, an examination and a simple blood test will tell you what you need to know.
