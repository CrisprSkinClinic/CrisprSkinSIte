Acne scarring typically presents as one of three recognized patterns, often in combination rather than in isolation: icepick scars, which are narrow and deep; boxcar scars, which are broader with sharply defined edges; and rolling scars, which are broad with sloping edges that give the skin an uneven, wave-like texture. Each pattern responds differently to available treatments, which is why [classifying the specific type](/blog/acne-scar-types-explained) -- or combination of types -- present matters more here than almost anywhere else in cosmetic dermatology.

A treatment that meaningfully improves rolling scars, for instance, may do very little for icepick scars, so [an accurate evaluation](/services/acne-scars) of your specific scarring pattern is the necessary first step before choosing a treatment course. Scars are best prevented by treating [acne](/conditions/acne-vulgaris) early.

## Telling the Types Apart

- **Icepick:** narrow (under 2 mm), deep, pit-like.
- **Boxcar:** round or oval with sharp edges, shallow to deep.
- **Rolling:** broad (4 to 5 mm), shallow waves caused by fibrous bands pulling the skin.
- **Hypertrophic and keloid:** raised scars, usually on the chest, back and jaw.

Most people have a mixture. A simple check: stretch the skin. Scars that flatten when stretched are generally easier to improve than those that stay, such as icepick scars.

## Why They Form

Acne inflammation damages collagen in the deeper skin. Picking, delayed treatment and severe cystic acne raise the risk. Early, effective acne treatment is the best prevention. See [acne treatment](/services/acne).

## Planning Treatment

Treatment begins with controlling acne, then matching methods to scar types, usually in combination over six to twelve months, and with realistic goals (a thirty to seventy percent improvement). See [acne scar treatment](/services/acne-scars).
