Infantile haemangiomas are common vascular birthmarks, sometimes called "strawberry marks," that typically appear within the first few weeks of life as a raised, red, rubbery growth. Most follow a predictable course: growing for several months, then gradually shrinking and fading over the following few years, often resolving largely on their own by early childhood.

For most infants, [monitoring through this natural course](/blog/birthmarks-for-parents) is the appropriate approach. Haemangiomas that grow especially rapidly, are unusually large, or are located near sensitive structures like the eyes or airway warrant [closer evaluation](/services/birthmarks), since these specific situations may benefit from treatment to prevent complications.

## What It Is

An infantile haemangioma, or strawberry mark, is a harmless collection of extra blood vessels. It appears in the first weeks of life as a pale or red patch, grows quickly for the first three to five months, then slowly shrinks over years. By age five to seven, many have faded, leaving loose skin, a faint mark or a scar.

## Most Need No Treatment

Small haemangiomas on the trunk or limbs are watched. But some need early treatment, because they can affect function or leave marks.

## When Early Treatment Matters

- Near the **eye, nose or mouth,** where they can interfere with vision, breathing or feeding.
- On the **lips, nose tip or ears,** where they can scar.
- **Large, multiple** or on the **liver or beard area,** which may signal internal problems.
- **Ulcerating** (open sores), which are painful.
- Those on the **face,** for appearance.

## Treatment

The mainstay is **propranolol**, a medicine given by mouth, which shrinks haemangiomas when started early, ideally in the first weeks, and monitored by a doctor. Topical timolol suits small, thin ones. Lasers may help ulcers and leftover redness. Surgery is reserved for selected cases.

## What Parents Should Do

Do not wait if a mark is growing near the eyes or mouth. Early review by a paediatric dermatologist makes a big difference. Photograph it weekly to follow growth. See our [birthmarks service](/services/birthmarks) and [pediatric dermatology](/services/pediatric-dermatology).
