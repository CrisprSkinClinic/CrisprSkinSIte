Paronychia is inflammation of the skin fold around the [nail](/services/nail-disorders). The acute form appears suddenly and is usually bacterial; the chronic form develops over weeks to months and is typically a barrier problem aggravated by moisture and irritation, sometimes with yeast involved.

Chronic cases heal only when the nail fold is protected from repeated wetting and injury, so habit changes are as important as medication. See also [nail fungus](/conditions/onychomycosis) and [ingrown toenails](/conditions/ingrown-toenail), which can look similar.

## Acute and Chronic Paronychia

**Acute paronychia** comes on over a day or two, with a red, warm, painful swelling beside the nail, sometimes with pus. It usually follows a small injury, nail biting, a manicure or pulling a hangnail, letting bacteria in.

**Chronic paronychia** develops over weeks to months, with a swollen, red, boggy nail fold, loss of the cuticle and ridged or discoloured nails. The cause is usually constant contact with water or irritants, with yeast and bacteria joining in. It is common in people who do wet work, such as dishwashers, cooks, cleaners and nurses, and in those with diabetes.

## Treatment

- **Acute:** warm soaks several times a day, antibiotics when needed, and drainage of pus by a doctor. Do not squeeze or cut it yourself.
- **Chronic:** keep the hands dry, wear cotton-lined waterproof gloves for wet work, apply a protective barrier cream, and use anti-inflammatory and antifungal treatment as advised. It takes weeks to months for the nail fold to heal.

## Prevention

Do not bite nails or pick cuticles, avoid aggressive manicures and cuticle cutting, keep hands dry and moisturised, and treat athlete's foot and other infections.

## When to See a Doctor

See a doctor for pus, spreading redness, fever or a swollen nail fold lasting more than two weeks. People with diabetes should be seen early. See [nail disorders](/services/nail-disorders) and [nail changes: when to see a doctor](/blog/nail-changes-when-to-see-a-doctor).
