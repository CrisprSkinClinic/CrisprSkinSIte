Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation refers to dark marks that develop at the site of a healed pimple, injury, or other skin inflammation, as the skin produces extra pigment in response to the healing process. It's especially common and visible in darker skin types, and while it often fades on its own over several months, [treatment](/services/pigmentation) can meaningfully speed this process along for marks that are slow to resolve.

The most effective preventive step is [treating active acne](/conditions/acne-vulgaris) or skin inflammation promptly, since the marks develop as a direct response to inflammation -- reducing how much inflammation occurs in the first place reduces how much post-inflammatory pigmentation follows. Melasma behaves differently; see [pigmentation vs. melasma](/blog/pigmentation-vs-melasma).

## What It Is

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) is flat brown or grey-brown discolouration left after skin inflammation has healed, such as after acne, eczema, insect bites, burns, rashes or procedures. Brown and darker skin is especially prone, because it makes more pigment when irritated. See [post-acne marks versus true scars](/blog/red-and-brown-marks-after-acne-vs-true-scars).

## How Long Does It Last?

Often six to twelve months, sometimes longer, especially if the original inflammation continues or the area is picked or sun-exposed. Marks deeper in the skin fade more slowly.

## Treatment

1. **Treat the cause** (acne, eczema) so that no new marks form.
2. **Strict sun protection every day,** including tinted sunscreen, since sun darkens marks.
3. **Brightening ingredients:** azelaic acid, niacinamide, vitamin C, retinoids and prescription creams, used under advice.
4. **Gentle chemical peels,** in a series. See [chemical peels](/services/chemical-peels).
5. **Avoid** picking, scrubbing and strong home bleaching products, which can worsen pigment.

## What to Expect

Marks fade gradually, usually by thirty to fifty percent with treatment over a few months, and may need maintenance.

## When to See a Doctor

See a dermatologist if marks are spreading, symmetrical on the face (possible melasma, see [melasma](/conditions/melasma)) or not improving. See [pigmentation](/services/pigmentation).
