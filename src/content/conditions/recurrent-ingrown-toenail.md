An ingrown toenail that settles and then returns, again and again, is frustrating, and it is common. For many people the first episode responds to simple measures, but if the nail edge is the underlying problem, those measures only buy time. Understanding why it recurs helps decide what to do about it.

## Why It Keeps Coming Back

**The edge of the nail keeps growing the same way.** If the nail is naturally curved, thick, or wide relative to the nail fold, its side edge will keep pressing into the skin as it grows, however carefully it is trimmed.

**Removing the edge without treating the growth point.** When only the offending sliver of nail is removed, the matrix at the side of the nail can regrow it, often with a sharp spike that digs in again. Treating that growth point is what stops it.

**Trimming and footwear habits.** Cutting nails too short, rounding the corners, or wearing tight shoes encourages the nail to dig in. See our advice on [preventing ingrown toenails](/blog/ingrown-toenail-recurrence-prevention-and-when-to-operate).

**Repeated injury or infection.** Each episode of inflammation and swollen tissue makes the next more likely, and chronic overgrown tissue can keep the cycle going.

## When a Procedure Makes Sense

If the toe has settled and flared repeatedly, or a previous simple removal of the edge has failed, a definitive approach is usually reasonable. That is [partial nail avulsion with lateral chemical matricectomy](/services/nail-avulsion-lateral-matricectomy): the problem strip of nail is removed and its growth point is treated so it does not regrow. It is done under local anaesthetic and recovery takes several weeks. See [what lateral chemical matricectomy involves](/blog/lateral-chemical-matricectomy-explained).

## What We Check First

We examine the nail, the surrounding skin, and the pressure points, look for infection or overgrown tissue, and review your circulation and general health. Diabetes, poor circulation, and active infection change the plan, and infection is dealt with first. Where the real problem is another nail condition such as a fungal infection, we address that too; see [nail fungus](/conditions/onychomycosis) and [paronychia](/conditions/paronychia).

## What Helps Afterwards

Even after a successful procedure, good habits protect the result: cut straight across, not too short; avoid tight or pointed shoes; and keep the toe clean and dry. These steps also help if you have a mild flare and prefer not to proceed.

## Realistic Expectations

The procedure substantially lowers the chance that the same edge returns, but no treatment makes recurrence impossible. The treated side of the nail will be a little narrower. We explain the benefits, risks, and alternatives before you decide, so you can choose what suits you.
