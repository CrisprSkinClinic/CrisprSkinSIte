Urticaria, commonly known as hives, is classified as acute (lasting under six weeks, often linked to an infection, food, or medication) or chronic (lasting six weeks or more, frequently without an identifiable external trigger) -- and this distinction meaningfully changes both the evaluation and treatment approach.

[Acute urticaria](/blog/hives-when-to-worry) is common and often resolves either on its own or with straightforward antihistamine treatment. [Chronic urticaria](/conditions/antihistamine-resistant-urticaria) is a different picture -- often without a clear external cause, sometimes linked to underlying autoimmune activity, and generally requiring a more structured, sustained management approach, including antihistamine doses higher than standard over-the-counter recommendations in many cases.

## Acute and Chronic Hives

**Acute hives** last less than six weeks. They commonly follow a viral infection, a food, an insect sting or a medicine, and usually settle on their own. **Chronic hives** last more than six weeks, come and go, and in most people have no outside trigger. They are often autoimmune in nature, and are not an allergy in the usual sense.

## What Hives Look Like

Raised, itchy welts of varying size that appear suddenly and fade within 24 hours, without leaving marks. Some people also have swelling of the lips, eyelids or hands (angioedema).

## Triggers That Can Worsen Hives

Aspirin and anti-inflammatory painkillers, heat, pressure, exercise, stress, alcohol and infections. Food is the cause in only a small share of cases. See [hives and food](/blog/hives-and-food-do-low-histamine-diets-work).

## Tests

For most people with chronic hives, only a few blood tests are needed. Wide allergy testing is not helpful without a clear history.

## Treatment

1. A **non-sedating antihistamine** taken daily, at higher doses under supervision if needed.
2. **Add-on treatments** when antihistamines are not enough. See [antihistamine-resistant urticaria](/conditions/antihistamine-resistant-urticaria).
3. **Short steroid courses** only for severe flares.

## When to Seek Emergency Help

Swelling of the tongue or throat, wheeze, dizziness or collapse. See [hives: when to worry](/blog/hives-when-to-worry) and our [urticaria service](/services/urticaria).
