Vitiligo is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system targets and destroys melanocytes, the cells responsible for skin pigment, causing well-defined patches of depigmented skin. It can occur anywhere on the body and affects people of all ages and skin tones, though it is often more noticeable and more distressing in deeper skin tones. Roughly one in a hundred people are affected worldwide, and it usually begins in childhood or young adulthood.

## What Vitiligo Looks Like

Patches are smooth, milk-white, and sharply bordered, and are not scaly, itchy, or painful in most people. Common sites include the face (around the eyes and mouth), the hands and fingers, the feet, the elbows and knees, and the genitals. Hair growing within a patch may turn white, a feature that has implications for treatment. Some people notice that new patches appear at sites of injury, friction, or sunburn, known as the Koebner phenomenon.

## Types of Vitiligo

**Non-segmental vitiligo** is the commonest type, with patches that often appear symmetrically on both sides of the body and tend to progress and sometimes recur over years. **Segmental vitiligo** is limited to one area or segment of the body, tends to start early in life, spreads for a limited period, and then often stabilises. Other, rarer patterns exist, including involvement of a limited number of patches or of mucosal sites such as the lips and genitals.

## Why It Happens

Genetics, the immune system, and environmental triggers all appear to play a part. Vitiligo runs in some families and is more common in people with other autoimmune conditions, such as thyroid disease, though most people with vitiligo have no family history. In many patients there is no identifiable trigger. It is not contagious, not caused by diet, and not caused by anything the patient has done.

## Active or Stable?

One of the most important questions at a first visit is whether the disease is currently active, with new or enlarging patches, or stable, with no change over a sustained period. This shapes the treatment order, and determines whether surgery is an option. We assess it using your history, examination, photographs, and often a Wood's lamp, and we reassess over time. See our page on stable versus active vitiligo for more detail.

## Diagnosis

Vitiligo is usually diagnosed by examination. A Wood's lamp, which shines ultraviolet light on the skin, helps confirm depigmentation and reveals the full extent, especially in lighter skin. Other conditions can look similar, including fungal infection (pityriasis versicolor), post-inflammatory pigment loss, and other causes of pale patches, so confirming the diagnosis matters. Because vitiligo is sometimes associated with thyroid and other autoimmune conditions, we may arrange basic blood tests.

## Treatment Overview

There is currently no guaranteed cure, but treatment can meaningfully restore pigment in many cases, particularly with early and consistent treatment. The options include topical corticosteroids and tacrolimus-type creams, newer topical agents where approved and available, narrowband UVB phototherapy and targeted excimer light, short courses of oral medicine for rapidly spreading disease, and surgical repigmentation for stable patches that have not responded. Supportive options include sun protection, cosmetic camouflage, and in selected cases medical tattooing. For very extensive vitiligo, where repigmentation is unrealistic, lightening the remaining skin is discussed only after other options and careful reflection.

## Where Treatment Works Best

Response varies by body area. The face and neck respond best, followed by the trunk and upper arms, while hands, fingers, feet, and areas over bony prominences respond least reliably. Recent patches respond better than long-standing ones, and patches with white hair, which suggests loss of the pigment reserve in the follicle, tend to respond more slowly. Treatment is usually continued for many months before judging response, and we review progress at regular intervals with photographs.

## Living With Vitiligo

Vitiligo rarely causes physical symptoms, but the emotional effect can be significant. Many people feel self-conscious, avoid certain clothing or social situations, or experience anxiety or low mood. This is common, and it deserves attention. Protecting depigmented skin from the sun with daily sunscreen is important, since it burns easily. Cosmetic camouflage is a legitimate option for those who want it, alongside medical treatment, and counselling or support groups can help.

## Realistic Expectations

We are candid from the first visit. Vitiligo is manageable, results vary, and it can recur after treatment, so follow-up matters. We explain what we expect for your specific pattern, rather than offering a generic timeline, and we will not promise outcomes we cannot reliably deliver.
