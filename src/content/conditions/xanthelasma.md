Xanthelasma are yellowish plaques that develop on or around the eyelids. They are made of cholesterol-laden cells that collect in the skin. They are the most common type of xanthoma, a skin sign of fat deposits, and they are benign, so they do not turn cancerous or affect vision. They do, however, tend to persist and slowly grow.

## What Causes Them

In a proportion of people, xanthelasma are linked to raised blood cholesterol or other lipid abnormalities, including inherited conditions that cause very high cholesterol from a young age. In others, blood lipids are normal and the cause is not clear. Diabetes, thyroid disease, and some liver conditions can contribute. Because of this variability, a blood test is the only way to know. See [xanthelasma and the cholesterol link](/blog/xanthelasma-what-it-is-and-the-cholesterol-link).

## Why a Medical Check Matters

Raised cholesterol increases the long-term risk of heart disease and stroke. Xanthelasma, especially at a young age or with a family history, can be an early visible clue. Checking and treating the lipids matters more for your health than removing the patches. Removing patches without addressing the lipids also makes recurrence more likely.

## What They Look Like

Typically soft, yellow or orange-yellow, flat or slightly raised, with a smooth surface and defined edges. They begin small, often at the inner upper eyelid, and can enlarge or multiply. They are usually symmetrical. They are painless and do not itch.

## Telling Them Apart From Other Lesions

Several things can look similar, including cysts, [skin tags](/conditions/skin-tags), warts, and, rarely, other growths. If a lesion is hard, ulcerated, bleeding, rapidly changing, or not yellow, an examination is important to rule out other diagnoses.

## Treatment Overview

Patches do not usually disappear on their own, and creams do not remove them. Treatment is considered for appearance or when patches are enlarging. We offer three established methods: a TCA touch for small, flat patches, surgical excision for larger or deeper ones, and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) for small to moderate patches. Each has advantages and drawbacks, and eyelid skin is thin, so careful technique matters. See [our treatment options compared](/blog/xanthelasma-treatment-options-compared) and the [xanthelasma treatment service](/services/xanthelasma-treatment).

## Recurrence

Recurrence is common after any treatment, sometimes within a year or two, particularly if the lipids remain high. Managing the underlying cause helps. See [recurrence and aftercare](/blog/xanthelasma-recurrence-and-aftercare).

## When to Seek Help

See us if you notice yellowish eyelid patches, particularly if they are new, increasing, or if you have a family history of high cholesterol or heart disease. Seek assessment promptly for any eyelid lump that is bleeding, ulcerated, painful, or rapidly growing.
