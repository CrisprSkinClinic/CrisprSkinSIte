"What should I eat for my skin?" is one of the questions we are asked most. Social media is full of confident answers. The research is more modest and more useful: **a few dietary links are real, many are exaggerated, and some popular restrictions are harmful.**

## What the Evidence Supports

- **Acne:** diets with a high glycaemic load are linked to worse acne, and some people notice a link with milk, particularly skimmed milk, and with whey protein supplements. See [food and acne](/blog/food-and-acne-what-the-evidence-says).
- **Eczema:** food allergy is a trigger in a minority, mainly young children with more severe eczema. Cutting out foods without proof does more harm than good. See [food allergy and eczema](/blog/food-allergy-and-eczema-when-to-test-and-when-not-to).
- **Psoriasis:** weight loss in people who are overweight, and less alcohol, help. Gluten-free diets help only in those with coeliac disease or related antibodies. See [psoriasis and diet](/blog/psoriasis-diet-weight-alcohol-gluten-evidence).
- **Hair:** iron deficiency, very low protein intake, and crash diets can cause shedding. See [nutrition and hair loss](/blog/nutrition-and-hair-loss-iron-vitamin-d-protein).

## What the Evidence Does Not Support

- That milk, fish, curd or citrus cause or worsen [vitiligo](/blog/vitiligo-diet-myths-milk-fish-citrus).
- That "detox" diets treat skin disease.
- That low-histamine diets reliably help [chronic hives](/blog/hives-and-food-do-low-histamine-diets-work).
- That high-dose biotin or other supplements grow hair in people who are not deficient. See [skin and hair supplements](/blog/supplements-for-skin-and-hair-what-helps-and-what-does-not).
