An ingrown toenail happens when the edge of the nail grows into the skin beside it, causing pain, swelling, and sometimes infection. Early cases often settle with good nail care. When the problem is established, keeps coming back, or is too painful to manage at home, a procedure that deals with the cause is usually the most reliable solution.

## What the Procedure Involves

The procedure combines two steps. In a **partial nail avulsion**, the offending strip of nail at the side of the toe is removed under local anaesthetic, which relieves the pressure straight away. In **lateral chemical matricectomy**, the small area of nail-forming tissue (the matrix) that would regrow that strip is treated with a chemical, so the problem edge does not grow back. Doing both together is what makes recurrence much less likely than removing the nail edge alone. We cover this in detail in our guide to [lateral chemical matricectomy](/blog/lateral-chemical-matricectomy-explained).

## Is It Right for You?

It is generally considered for ingrown toenails that are persistent, recurrent, or moderately to severely painful, particularly where the edge has repeatedly returned despite trimming or previous partial removal. We assess infection first, since active infection is treated before or alongside the procedure, and we review circulation, diabetes, and other health conditions that affect healing. See [ingrown toenails](/conditions/ingrown-toenail) and [recurrent ingrown toenails](/conditions/recurrent-ingrown-toenail) for how we assess them.

## What to Expect on the Day

The toe is numbed with a local anaesthetic injection at its base, which is the most uncomfortable part, and after that you should feel pressure but not pain. The procedure itself is short, and most people walk out the same day with a dressing and clear instructions. Our article on [what to expect before and after ingrown toenail surgery](/blog/ingrown-toenail-surgery-what-to-expect) walks through the day and the recovery.

## Recovery and the Result

Expect some drainage and tenderness over the following weeks as the toe heals, with dressing changes and protection from pressure. Most people return to ordinary activities within days, with a longer wait before strenuous exercise or tight shoes. The nail will be slightly narrower at the treated side, which is the trade-off for a much lower chance of the problem returning.

## Alternatives

Alternatives include continued conservative care, partial avulsion without the chemical step, or, in rare cases, more extensive surgery. We explain the benefits and drawbacks of each, including how likely the problem is to return, so you can choose what suits you.

## Realistic Expectations

No procedure removes every possibility of recurrence, and healing takes several weeks. We explain risks such as infection, delayed healing, and a narrowed nail before you decide, and we tell you plainly when we think the procedure is not the right choice for you yet. ## Why Surgery Rather Than Repeated Trimming

An ingrown edge that keeps coming back, or that has become infected, rarely settles with home care. Removing only the ingrown edge, and treating the matching nail root, prevents that edge from growing back. See [infected ingrown toenail: when to act](/blog/ingrown-toenail-infection-pus-when-to-see-a-doctor).

## The Steps

1. The toe is numbed with a ring block, and a tight band controls bleeding.
2. The ingrown strip of nail is separated and removed.
3. A small amount of chemical (phenol) is applied to the nail root on that side, then neutralised.
4. A dressing is applied. The procedure takes twenty to thirty minutes.

## Recovery

Wear an open shoe for a few days, change dressings as shown, and expect clear or blood-stained drainage for one to three weeks. Most people return to normal footwear in one to two weeks. See [recovery week by week](/blog/nail-avulsion-recovery-week-by-week).

## Results

Most people have no further trouble. The nail is slightly narrower on the treated side, which is intended. A small percentage have a recurrence, which can be treated again. See [ingrown toenails: when to operate](/blog/ingrown-toenail-recurrence-prevention-and-when-to-operate).

## Risks

Infection, delayed healing, a rough nail edge, and recurrence. People with diabetes, poor circulation or reduced foot sensation need extra care and follow-up.
