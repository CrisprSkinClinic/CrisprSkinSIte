Rosacea is a long-lasting inflammatory condition of facial skin. It affects adults, usually after thirty, and is often missed or confused with acne, sensitive skin or an allergy. In brown skin the redness can be harder to see, and the diagnosis is often delayed.

## What Rosacea Looks Like

- **Flushing:** the face turns red and hot easily, with heat, spicy food, alcohol, exercise or emotion.
- **Persistent redness** over the cheeks, nose, chin and forehead.
- **Small visible blood vessels.**
- **Red bumps and pimples,** without the blackheads of acne.
- **Burning, stinging and sensitivity** to products.
- **Eye symptoms:** dry, gritty, red or watery eyes, and swollen eyelids. See [rosacea and the eyes](/blog/rosacea-eyes-flushing-and-when-to-see-a-doctor).
- **Thickened skin of the nose** in advanced, long-standing disease, more often in men.

## What Triggers It

Sun, heat, spicy food, hot drinks, alcohol, stress, exercise, cold wind and some skin products. Triggers differ between people, so a short diary helps. See [rosacea triggers and skin care](/blog/rosacea-triggers-and-everyday-skin-care).

## Treatment

Rosacea cannot be cured, but it can be controlled.

1. **Gentle routine and strict sun protection.**
2. **Topical treatment:** azelaic acid, metronidazole and ivermectin for bumps, and brimonidine or oxymetazoline gels for redness.
3. **Tablets:** low-dose doxycycline or similar for moderate to severe or eye disease.
4. **Light and laser** for persistent redness and visible vessels.
5. **Isotretinoin** in low doses for severe, resistant disease, under close supervision.

See [rosacea treatment options](/blog/rosacea-treatment-options-creams-tablets-and-laser).

## What to Avoid

Steroid creams on the face, which can cause and worsen rosacea, harsh scrubs and many acne products, and known triggers.

## Is It Acne?

The two can look alike, and can coexist, but treatments differ. See [rosacea or acne?](/blog/rosacea-or-acne-or-seborrhoeic-dermatitis-how-to-tell).

## What to Expect

Most people improve in six to twelve weeks of treatment, and then control it with a maintenance routine. Flares can return, and early treatment keeps them mild.
