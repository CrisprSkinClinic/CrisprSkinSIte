Vitiligo surgery moves healthy pigment-producing cells from normal skin to a white patch. It does not cure vitiligo, and it is not for everyone. Where it is suitable, it can restore colour to patches that have not improved with medicines and light treatment.

## Who Qualifies?

Surgery works best in **stable** vitiligo: no new patches, no spreading, and no change in the existing patches for about a year, sometimes longer. Segmental vitiligo, which tends to stabilise early, often responds well. Active vitiligo, with new or growing patches, is likely to relapse after surgery, and a fresh surgical wound can itself trigger new patches. Read more in [who is a good candidate for vitiligo surgery](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-who-is-a-candidate) and [stable or active vitiligo?](/blog/stable-or-active-vitiligo-why-it-matters).

Surgery is also less suitable for people who form keloids, for very large areas and for patches that have little skin left to graft onto, such as the fingertips and lips, which are harder to treat.

## The Surgical Options

All options use local anaesthetic, and all take pigment cells from a normal part of the body, usually the thigh or buttock.

- **Non-cultured epidermal cell suspension:** cells are separated from a thin skin shaving, mixed into a liquid and spread over the patch, which has first been gently prepared. It can treat larger areas using a small donor patch.
- **Suction blister grafting:** a suction cup raises blisters on normal skin, and the blister roofs are placed on the prepared patch. Very thin, so it gives a good colour match, but it takes time to raise the blisters.
- **Punch grafting:** tiny cylinders of normal skin are placed in the patch. Cheap and simple, but it can leave a cobblestone look and is best for small patches.

We pick the method by site, size and your skin, and explain why. See [grafting options compared](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-grafting-options-compared).

## Recovery

The donor and treated areas are dressed for about a week, and the treated patch must be kept still and dry. Most people resume light activity within days. Repigmentation starts after several weeks, spreading from the grafted cells, and continues over three to six months. Sun protection and ongoing treatment help. See the [recovery timeline](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-recovery-and-aftercare-timeline).

## Results

Results vary with the method, the site and the stability of the vitiligo. Many well-selected patients see good repigmentation, particularly on the face, trunk and limbs. Hands and feet, and bony areas, respond less well. Some patches repigment partly, some match the surrounding skin imperfectly, and a few do not respond. We give an honest estimate at consultation, and a test patch is often wise. See [success rates and what to expect](/blog/vitiligo-surgery-success-rates-and-expectations).

## Risks

Infection, scarring at the donor or recipient site, a colour mismatch (too light or too dark), a cobblestone texture, and the appearance of new patches are possible. Careful selection and technique reduce them.

## What to Expect at Your Visit

We examine your patches with a special light, review the history of your vitiligo and earlier treatment, judge stability, and discuss whether surgery or more medical treatment should come first. Not everyone is advised to proceed, and we say so.
