Vitiligo is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system targets and destroys melanocytes, the cells responsible for skin pigment, leading to well-defined patches of depigmented skin. It can appear anywhere on the body, and its course varies significantly between individuals -- some people have a few small, stable patches for years, while others experience more extensive or actively spreading depigmentation.

We're candid from the first visit: vitiligo is a manageable condition, and treatment can meaningfully restore pigment in many cases, especially with early and consistent treatment, but there is currently no guaranteed cure, and results vary by body area, extent, and how active the condition is. We won't promise outcomes we can't reliably deliver.

## What to Expect at Your Visit

We examine the affected areas, often using a Wood's lamp to confirm the diagnosis and assess the full extent of involvement, since some patches aren't easily visible under normal light. We'll also ask about how long you've had it, whether it's been stable or spreading, and screen for other autoimmune conditions that occur somewhat more often alongside vitiligo, such as thyroid disease.

## Treatment Approach

Treatment depends on three things: the type of vitiligo, how much of the body is involved, and whether it is currently active or stable. We work through the options in a sensible order rather than offering everything at once.

**Active, spreading vitiligo.** The first aim is to halt progression, using phototherapy and topical treatment, and in rapidly progressing cases a short, supervised course of oral medicine.

**Limited patches.** Topical treatment is usually the starting point. Steroid creams are used in short supervised courses, while tacrolimus-type creams are preferred for the face, neck, and skin folds because they do not thin the skin. Newer topical agents are discussed where they are approved and available.

**Extensive vitiligo.** Narrowband UVB phototherapy is the established first-line option, with targeted excimer light for localised patches.

**Stable patches that have not responded.** Surgical repigmentation, including grafting and cell-transfer techniques, is considered once disease has been stable for a sustained period. Segmental vitiligo often responds particularly well.

**Supportive care.** Daily sun protection, cosmetic camouflage, and in selected cases medical tattooing are offered alongside medical treatment. For very extensive disease, where repigmentation is unrealistic, lightening the remaining skin is discussed only after other options and careful reflection.

We are candid about each option's benefits, limits, and time frame, so you can decide what is right for you.

## Setting Realistic Expectations

Response to treatment varies meaningfully by body area (the face generally responds best, hands and feet the least reliably), by how long a patch has been present, and by how active the disease currently is. We discuss this honestly and specifically for your case at your evaluation, rather than offering a generic timeline, so you can make an informed decision about which treatments to pursue.

## Diet Myths

There is no good evidence that milk, fish or citrus affect vitiligo. See [vitiligo and diet](/blog/vitiligo-diet-myths-milk-fish-citrus) and [vaccines and vitiligo](/blog/vaccines-and-psoriasis-vitiligo-alopecia-flares-myths).
