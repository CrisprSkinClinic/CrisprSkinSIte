Xanthelasma are soft, yellowish patches that appear on or around the eyelids, most often near the inner corner. They are collections of fat (cholesterol) deposited in the skin. They are not cancerous, do not harm the eye or vision, and are not painful, but they tend to persist and slowly grow, and many people want them treated for appearance.

## Why They Deserve a Medical Check

Xanthelasma can be a sign of raised cholesterol or another lipid disorder, though many people with them have normal blood tests. For that reason, we usually recommend a lipid profile, and we review risk factors for heart disease. Removing the patches without checking the cause misses the part that matters most for your health. See [xanthelasma and cholesterol](/blog/xanthelasma-what-it-is-and-the-cholesterol-link).

## Treatment Options

Several options exist, and no single one suits everyone. Choice depends on the size, depth, and position of the patches, your skin type, and how likely you are to tolerate downtime and scarring.

**Surgical excision.** The patch is cut out and the skin closed with fine stitches. It works well for larger or deeper patches and tends to give more lasting results, but it needs skill near the eyelid to avoid pulling the lid.

**Chemical peel.** A strong acid is applied to the patch in a controlled way, removing it gradually. It is suitable for small, flat patches and can be repeated, but it carries a risk of pigment change and, if too deep, scarring.

**Laser or radiofrequency ablation.** The patch is removed layer by layer with energy. It allows careful depth control, though there can be temporary redness and pigment change.

**Cryotherapy and electrosurgery.** Freezing or electrical destruction can treat small patches, used selectively on eyelid skin because of the risk of pigment change.

We compare these in more detail in [xanthelasma treatment options compared](/blog/xanthelasma-treatment-options-compared).

## Risks to Understand

Eyelid skin is thin, so treatments need care. Possible problems include scarring, lightening or darkening of the skin, and, with larger excisions, pulling of the eyelid. Recurrence is common after any method, so we discuss this honestly. See [recurrence and aftercare](/blog/xanthelasma-recurrence-and-aftercare).

## What to Expect at Your Visit

We examine the patches, ask about your family history and general health, and arrange blood tests if you have not had them. We explain which treatments suit you, the likely number of sessions, healing, and the chance of return, and we tell you when it is sensible to wait or to treat the cholesterol first.

## Realistic Expectations

Treatment can remove the patches well, but new ones can form, especially if the underlying cause is not addressed. Treating raised cholesterol and looking after your general health may reduce the risk of recurrence, and we coordinate that with your doctor where needed.
